# Progression and Gating Design for Gradually Superhuman Player Characters

Research notes compiled 2026-09-27. Scope: reproducible progression patterns, pacing numbers and design principles from shipped games and design theory, for a first-person physics-driven (Jolt) game whose player slowly becomes superhuman and whose powers are gated by their consequences. Candidate ability ladders appear in the section "Candidate ability ladders" and are proposals traced to sources, not final design.

## Superhero and power-growth games: how powers unlock, how consequences are taught, documented numbers, world reaction

### Takeaway
Shipped superhero games almost universally unlock movement powers on a short ladder of 4–6 tiers fed by a large, visible collectible economy (Crackdown: 500 orbs for 4 tiers; Crackdown 3: 750 orbs for 6; Saints Row IV: 1,255 clusters, 1,065 needed), and they order the ladder so that a raw capability (double jump, air dash, sprint speed) arrives before the ability that makes it safe or efficient (glide, stamina reduction, "Immovable Object", "Resilience"). Only a few (Dishonored, Breath of the Wild, Dark Souls, Death Stranding) keep real physical consequences; most superhero games remove fall damage entirely, which is the single biggest divergence from the user's brief.

### Inferences
- The industry-standard superhero ladder is 4–6 tiers per movement stat with roughly 100–150 collectibles per tier (Crackdown 500/4; Crackdown 3 750/6) and a deliberate over-supply (Crackdown "4 stars before you get them all"; Saints Row IV 190 surplus clusters) so completionism is optional, not required.
- Almost every source orders "raw capability → efficiency/safety": Prototype Airdash (2,500 EP) precedes Glide (15,000) and "Air Recovery" (30,000); Crackdown 3 gives Boosters (L1) before Air Dash (L2), and puts "Resilience" at Strength L4 after Ground Pound (L2) and Flying Fist (L3); Saints Row IV requires Air Dash before Glide and sells stamina cost reduction in 75%→50%→unlimited steps. This is the same shape as the user's "high jump before fall tolerance" rule, but the shipped games mostly *cap the consequence at zero* (Prototype: no fall damage; Saints Row IV first sprint upgrade is 90% damage reduction) rather than letting it kill.
- The games that keep physical consequences (Dishonored fall damage; BotW stamina exhaustion → fall/sink; Dark Souls roll length; Death Stranding balance) all pair the consequence with a *self-rescue verb* (Blink to a ledge; food-based temporary stamina; light equipment; hold to steady). That pairing, not the removal of consequence, is the reproducible pattern for the brief.
- Exposing internal tuning parameters in upgrade text (Saints Row IV: `super_lateral_velocity`, `glide_terminal_velocity`, `super_sprint_buildup_time`) is a shipped precedent for making per-tier multipliers legible and reproducible.

### Gaps
- No primary Realtime Worlds source gives the actual jump-height or speed multipliers per Crackdown tier; the "15/20/25/30 ft" figures are wiki-only, undated and unattributed. A Crackdown fall-damage behaviour source was not found (planned search blocked by budget exhaustion).
- Crackdown 2 per-level unlock thresholds (orbs-per-level) could not be verified: SuperCheats returned HTTP 403 and Wikipedia omits them.
- No developer statement was found on why Superman Returns moved the health bar to the city; only the mechanic is documented.
- Prototype 2's exact EP or mutation numbers beyond the "+25%" bonuses were not confirmed from a non-wiki source.
- Doug Sheahan's "Concrete Jungle Gym" talk content (numbers on swing physics assists) was not opened (YouTube blocked); only the session description was obtained.

## Consequence-based gating precedents: a power is dangerous until a second power tames it

### Takeaway
Direct precedents exist in three families: (1) "speed without brakes" (Just Cause 3's wingsuit can only stop by crashing/ragdolling, parachuting, or a purchased Gear Mod air brake; Titanfall's untrusted wall-run), (2) "height without a landing" (Dishonored's Blink-to-ledge cancelling fall damage; BotW's stamina running out mid-climb), and (3) "load without balance" (Death Stranding, Dark Souls equip load). The designers who kept the danger made it readable with a diegetic gauge, a telegraphing camera or animation change, and a cheap recovery verb, and in every case the danger is preventable by the player, matching Schell's rule that punishment must be "understood and preventable".

### Cited Findings
- Speed before brakes (literal): "Stopping while wingsuiting can only be done with Gear Mods. The two other options are to either crash into something and ragdoll, or switch to Parachute." The sequel then unlocked the brake for free: "The air brake feature no longer requires Gear Mods". (wiki-only) — [Wingsuit — Just Cause Wiki (Fandom)](https://justcause.fandom.com/wiki/Wingsuit)
- Speed before trust: players "wouldn't trust the wall-run and press the A button as quickly as they could, and they'd end up jumping right off it"; Respawn added a camera tilt "as players jump toward walls but before running begins" and enforced sequences; wall-running only became worth the risk once it "granted a speed boost". — [Designer Interview: Getting Titanfall's controls just right — Wiltshire, 2017, Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/designer-interview-getting-i-titanfall-i-s-controls-just-right)
- Height before landing: "casting Blink at a ledge so that the user will climb up upon arrival will cancel falling damage … it does not reset the fall counter." Blink is free to cast repeatedly (20% mana, regenerating). (wiki-only) — [Blink — Dishonored Wiki (Fandom)](https://dishonored.fandom.com/wiki/Blink); testers combined "a high jump with the ability to teleport" and Arkane "tried to design levels to accommodate them". — [Dishonored — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dishonored)
- Climb before endurance: exhaustion "disables him from certain actions, such as using the Paraglider or climbing", and without stamina Link "will instead sink into the water"; the gauge "appears near Link whenever he is performing an action that requires great effort". Nintendo cut a mid-climb rest mechanic (sword-stab) because it was implausible and could not be used "anywhere you want". (wiki-only for mechanics) — [Stamina Wheel — Zelda Wiki (Fandom)](https://zelda.fandom.com/wiki/Stamina_Wheel); [Siliconera, 2017 (GameSpot interview)](https://www.siliconera.com/breath-wild-cut-idea-made-possible-regain-stamina-climbing/)
- Load before balance: "Too much weight on Sam's back can throw off his balance, and heavier loads make movement more difficult"; cargo integrity is shown on "damage sensor tape … from yellow to red". (wiki-only) — [Cargo — Death Stranding Wiki (Fandom)](https://deathstranding.fandom.com/wiki/Cargo); Kojima: "You have to select one bottle when you climb a mountain." — [Game Informer, 2019](https://gameinformer.com/interview/2019/09/16/hideo-kojima-answers-our-questions-about-death-stranding)
- Load before agility: equip load >50% doubles roll duration (21–24 → 46–48 frames) while i-frames stay at 11, so the penalty is legible as recovery time, not as lost invulnerability. — [Equip Load — Fextralife](https://darksouls.wiki.fextralife.com/Equip_Load)
- Raw jump before air control: Crackdown 3 Agility L1 "Boosters … double-jump" precedes L2 "Air Dash … quickly change direction in the air". — [Shacknews, 2019](https://www.shacknews.com/article/109880/skills-abilities-and-leveling-fast-in-crackdown-3)
- Dash before glide, twice: Prototype "Glide – 15,000 EP … unlocked by buying Airdash" — [Altered Gamer](https://www.alteredgamer.com/prototype/39802-powers-movement-and-survivability-guide/); Saints Row IV "Use Sprint after an Air Dash to Glide. (Requires Air Dash)" (wiki-only) — [Saints Row Wiki](https://saintsrow.fandom.com/wiki/Super_Powers_in_Saints_Row_IV)
- Knockback before recovery: Prototype sells "Air Recovery – 30,000 EP – You can regain control when knocked through the air" as a separate purchase after the movement powers that get you knocked around. — [Altered Gamer](https://www.alteredgamer.com/prototype/39802-powers-movement-and-survivability-guide/)
- Fall protection first (counter-example): A Story About My Uncle starts the suit with "shock-absorbing boots that prevent damage from long falls", adds "jet-propelled boots" later, and "does not feature a failure state such as losing lives or restarting levels". — [A Story About My Uncle — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_Story_About_My_Uncle)
- Inertia deliberately removed (counter-example): Mirror's Edge "is not meant to be a simulation"; players can "stop quickly after a full sprint because the development team felt that keeping the inertia would make the gameplay irritating", even though momentum "preserving it through multiple obstacles allows the player to run faster, jump farther and climb higher". — [Mirror's Edge — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mirror%27s_Edge)
- Physics puzzle "set by reality" (KSP): IGN's Seth G. Macy, "By the time I finally built a rocket that achieved successful orbit, I had failed so many times that in almost any other game I would have given up completely"; Edge: "you'll crack a puzzle set by reality." — [Kerbal Space Program — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kerbal_Space_Program)
- Fairness rule: punishment should be "understood and preventable"; "Games that are fun have elements of high-risk / high-reward options." — [The Art of Game Design (2nd ed.) notes — Jesse Schell via notesbylex](https://notesbylex.com/the-art-of-game-design-a-book-of-lenses-2nd-edition-by-jesse-schell)
- Information and feedback rule: "It's almost worth erring on the side of providing the player with too much information"; "There's nothing more paranoia-inducing than having made a decision and the game just kind of goes on." — [GDC 2012: Sid Meier on how to see games as sets of interesting decisions — Leigh Alexander, 2012, Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/gdc-2012-sid-meier-on-how-to-see-games-as-sets-of-interesting-decisions)

### Inferences
- The readable-and-fair recipe that recurs across these precedents: (a) a diegetic gauge that appears only during the risky action (BotW wheel, Death Stranding tape), (b) a pre-impact telegraph (Titanfall camera tilt; Dark Souls longer roll animation), (c) a cheap, always-available recovery verb (Blink, parachute, grab ledge), and (d) a purchasable *upgrade* that removes the need for the verb later (JC4 air brake; SR4 unlimited stamina; Prototype Air Recovery).
- Just Cause 3 → 4 is the cleanest "speed before brakes" case study: the studio first sold the brake as a mod, then made it standard, implying players found un-brakeable speed punishing rather than fun over a full game. For the brief, this argues for a short window (one tier) between "can go fast" and "can stop", not a long one.
- Mirror's Edge's decision to drop stopping inertia is the strongest warning sign: full inertia in first person was judged "irritating" by DICE. Since the brief wants inertia to *be* the fun, the counter-measures above (telegraphing, cheap self-arrest, Euphoria-style recovery) should be treated as mandatory, not optional.

## Metroidvania ability gating theory and lock-and-key literature

### Takeaway
The formal literature (Dormans) separates a level's *mission* (task graph) from its *space* (map) and treats abilities as keys that "transform a linear mission into a branching space"; the practitioner literature (Anthropy & Clark, Cook) adds that new verbs must be introduced in "safe areas" and that each skill is a feedback loop that must be mastered before the next. Mark Brown's Boss Keys popularised dependency-graph mapping of these gates, but its transcripts could not be opened here.

### Cited Findings
- Dormans' formal model: "A level's mission can be represented as a flowchart of the tasks and challenges players need to complete in order to finish the level. A level's space consists of its geographical layout"; "a complete level consists of both a space and a mission". Design principles such as "the use of locks and keys to transform a linear mission into a branching space" can be formalised as graph rewrite rules. Constraint example: "no designer would place a crucial key behind a lock opened by that same key, as this would create a deadlock." Reusing space "allows us to design or generate levels that are less linear and foreground the player's growing experience." — [Level Design as Model Transformation: A Strategy for Automated Content Generation — Joris Dormans, 2011, PCGames 2011 (ACM), Bordeaux](https://www.pcgworkshop.com/archive/dormans2011design.pdf)
- Earlier paper (not opened; abstract via Semantic Scholar): Dormans analysed a Twilight Princess dungeon into missions and spaces and proposed graph grammars for missions and shape grammars for spaces. — [Adventures in level design: generating missions and spaces for action adventure games — Dormans, 2010, PCGames workshop](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Adventures-in-level-design:-generating-missions-and-Dormans/7113e80618d405bc39d5cb1793fb9e9672b7ad54)
- Practitioner summary of lock-and-key (snippet): keys "can take the literal form of keys and lock doors, or a more abstract form, such as an item which has an effect on the environment in a way which makes new areas accessible." — [Lock and Key Dungeons — Boris the Brave, 2021](https://www.boristhebrave.com/2021/02/27/lock-and-key-dungeons/)
- Genre definition: "parts of the world will be inaccessible to the player until they acquire special items, tools, weapons, abilities, or knowledge"; improvements "aid the player in defeating more difficult enemies and locating shortcuts". Koji Igarashi on "designing maps that encourage exploration but which still guide the player on a main path … and providing means where the player can be aware of where they are". Thomas Mahler (Ori): "Everything is so interconnected that if you change one aspect of the game, it's just inevitable that it's going to influence the rest". — [Metroidvania — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metroidvania)
- Boss Keys: Mark Brown started the series in 2016 on Zelda dungeons, "developed a mapping system to describe layouts of the dungeons", and began a Metroidvania season in July 2018. — [Game Maker's Toolkit — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Game_Maker%27s_Toolkit); Super Metroid episode (Sept 2018) "included a full dependency graph" (search snippet; Patreon post returned 403, YouTube blocked) — [The World Design of Super Metroid — GMTK, 2018, Patreon/YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=nn2MXwplMZA); Metroid Prime episode dated 14 Mar 2019, with viewers noting that revisiting areas with new tools makes "the mastery you're feeling over the world feel more and more obvious on each new visit" (forum reactions only). — [ResetEra thread, 2019](https://www.resetera.com/threads/game-makers-toolkit-the-world-design-of-metroid-prime-boss-keys.105334/)
- Verbs, objects, safe areas: verbs are "the rules that give the player the freedom to change the game state"; designers should "work on developing existing verbs and objects fully" rather than adding many; level design uses "safe areas where the player is able to test out the limitations of her verbs, or able to observe the behaviour of certain objects … without being placed in immediate danger"; scenes are "the most basic unit of pacing" and "should be used to develop rules, and, most importantly, to introduce them"; "Resistance" is "the way a game pushes back on the player as they attempt to exert control over the game state." — [A Game Design Vocabulary (Anthropy & Clark, 2014) reviewed — Sebastian Atay, 2014, First Person Scholar](https://www.firstpersonscholar.com/a-game-design-vocabulary/)
- Skill atoms: each is a loop of Action → Simulation → Feedback → Modeling; "a highly mechanical and predictable heart, built on the foundation of basic human psychology, beats at the core of every single successful game"; "If they feel that their action has been in vain, they feel boredom or frustration." Three skill types: pre-existing, rewarded ("explicit reward and punishment metrics guiding the player"), emergent ("clever combinations of existing skills that the designer either did not reward or could not reward"). — [The Chemistry of Game Design — Daniel Cook, 2007, Lostgarden](https://lostgarden.com/2007/07/19/the-chemistry-of-game-design/); [same, Gamasutra/Game Developer, 2007](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/the-chemistry-of-game-design)

### Inferences
- Treat each superhuman tier as a *key* in Dormans' sense and each physical consequence as a *lock* on the same graph: "jump tier 2" opens rooftop routes (spatial lock) but also creates a "fall-death" lock that only "ledge grab" or "impact tolerance" opens. Dormans' deadlock rule then yields a hard constraint: never require a consequence-taming power to be reachable only via the consequence it tames.
- Anthropy & Clark's "safe area" plus Cook's "rewarded skill" imply a concrete introduction template per tier: a low-stakes scene where the new verb's failure is visible but survivable (water, soft debris, a ledge always in range) before the first lethal instance.

- Boss Keys' actual analysis text (Super Metroid's ability counts and gating order, Hollow Knight's Mantis Claw placement) was not obtained; Patreon (403), YouTube (redirect to Google consent), GMTK website (no transcripts). No Team Cherry or Moon Studios interview on gating was retrieved (search budget exhausted).
- Zelda dungeon design primary sources (Aonuma/Fujibayashi) were not searched.

## Fun theory: flow, learning, interesting decisions, juice, risk/reward, Schell's lenses

### Takeaway
The theory converges on three testable rules for a power ladder: keep challenge inside a per-player "fuzzy safe zone" between anxiety and boredom by embedding difficulty choices in play itself (Csikszentmihalyi via Chen); make each new power a fresh pattern to learn so mastery does not decay into boredom (Koster, Cook); and make every consequence a legible tradeoff with immediate feedback (Meier, Schell), amplified by "juice" so the physics reads instantly.

### Cited Findings
- Csikszentmihalyi's conditions: "The activity must have clear goals and progress"; "The task must provide clear and immediate feedback"; "Good balance is required between the perceived challenges of the task and one's perceived skills." Anxiety when challenge exceeds skill, boredom when skill exceeds challenge. Primary work: *Flow: The Psychology of Optimal Experience* (Harper & Row, 1990). — [Flow (psychology) — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flow_(psychology))
- Chen's thesis: "If the challenge is higher than the ability, the activity becomes overwhelming and generates anxiety. If the challenge is lower than the ability, it provokes boredom. Fortunately, human beings have tolerance, there is a fuzzy safe zone". Eight components of flow (challenging activity requiring skill, merging of action and awareness, clear goals, direct feedback, concentration, sense of control, loss of self-consciousness, transformation of time); "Not all of these components are needed". "like fingerprints, different people have different skills and Flow Zones." Sense of control matters as much as difficulty: "Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi often describes Flow as driving a small boat in parallel to the current." Methodology: "1. Expand your game's Flow coverage by including a wide spectrum of gameplay with different difficulties and flavors 2. Create a Player-oriented Active DDA system to allow different players to play in their own paces 3. Embed DDA choices into the core gameplay mechanics"; "The only solution is to embed choices into the gameplay, let the player treat choices as part of the play and eventually ignore them." — [Flow in Games — Jenova Chen, 2006/2007, MFA thesis, USC (PDF)](https://www.jenovachen.com/flowingames/Flow_in_games_final.pdf); short version — [Flow in games (and everything else) — Chen, 2007, Communications of the ACM 50(4)](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/1232743.1232769)
- Koster: fun is learning; games present "a problem … a topology … and a core mechanic. Then you get told how you did"; dopamine "specifically enhances learning and memory" and relates to "predicting rewarding outcomes"; "Art's challenging; art we have to work for". — [Raph Koster's Theory of Fun, ten years on — Leigh Alexander, 2012, Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/raph-koster-s-theory-of-fun-ten-years-on); book: *A Theory of Fun for Game Design* (Koster, 2004; 2nd ed. 2013); slides (not opened): [Theory of Fun: 10 Years Later — Koster, GDC Online 2012](https://www.raphkoster.com/gaming/gdco12/Koster_Raph_Theory_Fun_10.pdf)
- Meier's interesting decisions (GDC 2012): tradeoffs ("the opportunity to get a big sword costs 500 gold"); "Good decisions are situational"; decisions that "express your personal play style"; persistence ("affect the game for a certain amount of time"); risk-vs-reward as a universal category; feedback: "The worst thing you can do is just move on." — [GDC 2012: Sid Meier on how to see games as sets of interesting decisions — Leigh Alexander, 2012, Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/gdc-2012-sid-meier-on-how-to-see-games-as-sets-of-interesting-decisions); talk video — [Interesting Decisions — GDC Vault, 2012](https://gdcvault.com/play/1015756/Interesting)
- Juice: "Juice It Or Lose It" (Martin Jonasson and Petri Purho, GDC Europe 2012) demonstrates tweening, scaling, screen shake and particles on a Breakout clone; a 2014 rebuttal by Folmer Kelly warned that with excessive eye candy "the context doesn't get enough consideration". — [Video: Indies, resist the urge to 'juice it or lose it' — Game Developer, 2014](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/video-indies-resist-the-urge-to-juice-it-or-lose-it-); talk — [Juice It or Lose It — GDC Vault, 2012](https://www.gdcvault.com/play/1016487/juice-it-or-lose); [YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Fy0aCDmgnxg)
- Schell (2nd ed. summary): "flow activities must manage to stay in the narrow margin of challenge that lies between boredom and frustration"; Balance Type #2 Challenge vs Success ("increase difficulty with each success", "let skilled players skip easy sections", layered challenges); Lens of Punishment — punishment "creates endogenous value and excitement through risk" but "all punishment should be understood and preventable"; Lens of Rewards — players "acclimate quickly", use variable schedules and rising values; "Games that are fun have elements of high-risk / high-reward options"; Lens of Freedom / "indirect control" through constraints, goals, interface and visual guidance. — [The Art of Game Design: A Book of Lenses (2nd ed., Schell, 2014) — notes by Lex](https://notesbylex.com/the-art-of-game-design-a-book-of-lenses-2nd-edition-by-jesse-schell) (secondary summary; book: CRC Press)
- Iteration-time as anti-frustration (fast retry as reward): Team Meat "wanted the game to be rewarding and challenging, rather than frustrating; to this end they included infinite lives, quick restarts of levels, obvious goals, and short levels" and "felt the replay feature transformed death into a form of reward." — [Super Meat Boy — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Super_Meat_Boy); Celeste is "divided into multiple single-screen challenges, with checkpoints in between screens", "difficult yet fair", with Assist Mode added in "a couple of days' work". — [Celeste (video game) — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Celeste_(video_game))

### Inferences
- Chen's "embed choices into the gameplay" maps directly onto consequence-gated powers: the player chooses their own difficulty by choosing how high to jump or how fast to sprint *before* they own the taming power; the ladder therefore widens the flow zone without menus, provided the consequence is readable (Meier's information rule).
- Koster and Cook together predict a boredom cliff when a tier is fully mastered; the shipped ladders' 4–6 tiers with a new verb per tier (not just a multiplier) are consistent with staving that off. A pure "+5 ft per level" tier with no new verb (Crackdown 1's 5th star grants "no new abilities") is the pattern to avoid.
- Meier's "persistence" criterion supports letting a bad landing have lasting cost (damaged suit, limp, stunned Euphoria get-up) rather than instant reset — but Super Meat Boy and Celeste show that the *retry loop* must stay short even if the *consequence* is persistent.

- No specific GDC talk on "progression curves and reward schedules" or "mastery and progression" was retrieved (planned search blocked by budget exhaustion). Foddy's GDC frustration talk (see next section) could not be located on GDC Vault via the browse page.
- Schell and Koster were read via secondary summaries, not the books; chapter numbers are from the summary (Flow ch. 10; Skill ch. 12; Balance ch. 13; Indirect control ch. 18).

## Physics failure as fun (Human Fall Flat, Gang Beasts, Getting Over It, QWOP, Octodad, Goat Simulator; Euphoria)

### Takeaway
The physics-comedy designers agree on a small set of rules: the body should be a genuine simulation so failure is unique and legible ("slight differences in angle and thrust … vastly differing results"); the player should be given tight control of one anchor (Octodad's foot, Bob's hands, the hammer) while the rest flops; invisible helper forces should remove *unfair* snags; failure should be survivable and quickly retried; and comedy must come from the systems, not authored jokes. Foddy's counter-position — deliberately "greasy" controls and no checkpoints — works only because the loss is legible and the goal self-set.

### Cited Findings
- Foddy on why frustration is worth designing: "This is one of the things I'm most interested in. As a game designer, the most fundamental problem you're up against is that games don't matter, they're fake." On continuous loss of progress versus discrete death: "It's emotionally different to having to pick yourself up again." On choking: "I would feel myself tensing up and I'd be too careful and I'd tighten up and I'd lose it." On narration: "There's no completely non-annoying way of encouraging someone." — [Designer Interview: The aesthetics of frustration in Getting Over It — Alex Wiltshire, 2018, Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/designer-interview-the-aesthetics-of-frustration-in-i-getting-over-it-i-)
- Foddy: "More than anything else, what I love about games, what I love about making games, is griefing the player." In-game confession: "I could have made something you would have liked, a game that was empowering, that would save your progress and inch you steadily forward. Instead, I must confess: this isn't nice." His games use "fiddly controls" in high-stress situations to explore "the full space of how you can put hurdles in front of players"; he has "a taxonomy of frustration". — [Here's Why the Designer of 'QWOP' Likes Making People Mad — Cameron Kunzelman, 2017, Vice](https://www.vice.com/en/article/heres-why-the-designer-of-qwop-likes-making-people-mad/)
- Foddy: "I want to explore frustration; maybe it's richer and more interesting than people have given it credit for." "Frustration, confusion, any of the things that we'd construct as standing in the way of the function of a piece of software, are deemed bad in game design." "Art is a container to experience and express emotions that you don't want affecting your everyday life." — [Design Notes Episode 7: Bennett Foddy — Google Design, n.d.](https://design.google/library/bennett-foddy-getting-over-it-interview)
- Getting Over It structure: "there are no checkpoints"; falling loses progress; Foddy aimed the game at "a certain kind of person, to hurt them"; "The flavor of being sent back gradually disappeared" from games; inspired by Jazzuo's *Sexy Hiking* (2002). — [Getting Over It with Bennett Foddy — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Getting_Over_It_with_Bennett_Foddy)
- Why its failure is readable: "slight differences in factors like angle and thrust can result in vastly differing results"; the level has "outcroppings designed for you to hook onto when falling" that function "sort of like checkpoints", but late falls can pass them; the cauldron "keeps me from feeling that there are other actions that I should be able to take." — [Thoughts on Getting Over It — Nikhil Murthy, 2018, Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/thoughts-on-getting-over-it)
- QWOP (2008): ragdoll runner with independent thigh/calf keys; Foddy: "One of the things I found with QWOP is that people like to set their own goals in a game" — some are satisfied at 5 m; leaderboards would have driven casual players out. — [QWOP — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/QWOP)
- Octodad postmortem: "We needed them to be awkward without being frustrating." Fixes: increase foot speed and reduce its perceived weight for tight directional control while the body stays physics-driven; "Invisible helper forces prevented frustrating moments (legs catching on stairs or furniture)" via placed force volumes; late levels "over-emphasizing punishment with timed sequences and precision challenges" were patched into slapstick (a box disguise replaced stealth); "expertise blindness led developers to overestimate player readiness". — [Octodad: Dadliest Catch Post-Mortem Pt. 3: Design — Kevin Zuhn, 2015, Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/octodad-dadliest-catch-post-mortem-pt-3-design)
- Octodad's origin: *Jurassic Park: Trespasser*'s "granular limb interface had been the source of unintentional comedy" — the team asked whether it could be "harnessed for deliberate comedy" (snippet). — [Octodad devs reveal the unlikely inspiration for their comedy — Kill Screen, 2014](https://killscreen.com/articles/octodad-dadliest-catch-devs-reveal-unlikely-inspiration-their-comedy/)
- Human: Fall Flat — Sakalauskas on emergence: "It's all physics and engineering and so on, and you cannot base it on game design documents. You make systems, and from those systems, nice behaviors emerge." The cancelled sequel failed because "It was too stiff. Too polished." He prioritises "immersion and physical reactivity". — [Human Fall Flat 2 is cancelled … founder looks back on a defining decade — Chris Kerr, 2026, Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/production/-human-fall-flat-2-is-cancelled-we-are-making-human-fall-flat-3-no-brakes-games-founder-looks-back-on-a-defining-decade)
- Human: Fall Flat — playtest that set the design: his son "did everything possible not to solve puzzles" and instead enjoyed the physics, so puzzles became "not really watertight"; player-respecting rule: "If someone tried to do something in the game then either I would make it unattractive to try if it would be too difficult to implement, or I would make sure that it works as expected by the player." Early independent-arm controls were "really messy" and simplified. — [Human Fall Flat Interview With Tomas Sakalauskas — Dave Aubrey, 2021, TheGamer](https://www.thegamer.com/human-fall-flat-interview-tomas-sakalauskas/); [Human: Fall Flat — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human:_Fall_Flat) (58 million copies by Dec 2025)
- Human: Fall Flat on AAA avoidance (snippet): "physics can be a bit unpredictable at times" and "it's difficult to make really big triple-A productions relying on just physics because it could go totally sideways"; the fun comes from "slapstick physics". — [Human Fall Flat Developer Explains Why Big Games Opt For Simple Physics — TheGamer](https://www.thegamer.com/risky-human-fall-flat-big-games-simple-physics/)
- Goat Simulator: Ibrisagic "only sought to fix software bugs that might cause the game to crash, leaving in the other glitches and bugs associated with the physics engine as the results from these were 'really hilarious'"; prototype used "Nvidia PhysX and an Apex physics engine with ragdoll physics" in UE3, from a January 2014 one-month jam. — [Goat Simulator — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Goat_Simulator)
- Gang Beasts: "players fight each other as floppy, wiggly ragdolls"; knocked-down opponents "can fight back after they return to consciousness" (recovery built into the loop). — [Gang Beasts — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gang_Beasts). No Boneloaf design quotes were found in opened sources.
- Euphoria (the brief's reference for reactions): "based on a full simulation of the 3D character, including body, muscles and motor nervous system"; used in GTA IV, Red Dead Redemption, GTA V, RDR2, Max Payne 3, Backbreaker. — [Euphoria (software) — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euphoria_(software)); it "synthesize[s] interactive animations on the fly, using fast simulations of the game character's actual motor control, muscles and biomechanics" so "characters move and adapt realistically to their given situations on the fly, resulting in a series of unique experiences and payoffs" (Torsten Reil). — [Product: Grand Theft Auto IV Using NaturalMotion's Euphoria — Brandon Boyer, 2007, Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/game-platforms/product-i-grand-theft-auto-iv-i-using-naturalmotion-s-euphoria); self-preservation behaviours (protect head, break fall with hands, grab injured parts) are described in the GTA Wiki (snippet only, not opened) — [Euphoria — GTA Wiki (Fandom)](https://gta.fandom.com/wiki/Euphoria)

### Inferences
- Octodad's fix set is a direct template for a first-person physics character: one tightly controlled anchor (camera/head and hands), a floppy body, and designer-placed helper force volumes where snagging would be unfair. Human Fall Flat's rule ("make sure it works as expected by the player") is the acceptance criterion.
- Foddy's model (continuous loss, no checkpoints) is compatible with the brief only if the physics remains legible; his own commentary and Murthy's analysis attribute the tolerance of loss to *readability* (you can see why you fell) plus optional soft checkpoints (outcroppings). For a superhuman progression game, his QWOP finding — self-set goals — suggests letting players pick how far to push a dangerous power rather than mandating it.
- Sakalauskas' "too stiff, too polished" failure and Goat Simulator's kept bugs both argue that the *imperfection* of the simulation is the comedy; an Euphoria-style layer should therefore be allowed to fail visibly (stumbles, flails) rather than be blended toward canned animation.

### Gaps
- Foddy's GDC talk (title reported by a search snippet as "Getting Over It: The Design of Frustration", year given as 2019, unverified) could not be located on GDC Vault via the browse page fetched, and the Medium summary returned 403; no transcript was obtained.
- No quantitative "iteration time / respawn speed" data (seconds to retry) was found for any of these games; only qualitative statements (Super Meat Boy "quick restarts", Celeste per-screen checkpoints).
- Gang Beasts and Goat Simulator developer interviews were not opened (Boneloaf: none found; Coffee Stain: Wikipedia only).
- KSP developer Felipe Falanghe's "joy of failure" interview (PC Gamer) was paywalled; only reviewer quotes via Wikipedia and a snippet ("a series of small moments that would actually lift the always looming concern of impending failure").

## Skills that grow through use versus unlock points: fit for powers that "slowly develop"

### Takeaway
Three shipped models exist: (1) use-based growth with perk thresholds (Kingdom Come, Skyrim, Dying Light 2 parkour XP), (2) collectible-fed stat tiers (Crackdown, Saints Row IV), and (3) story-gated core powers with point-priced depth (Control, inFamous). Crackdown is the hybrid closest to the brief: the *method* of play grows the matching stat (kill from height → Agility), so the character becomes what the player does.

### Cited Findings
- Kingdom Come: "The game employs a classless system where skills improve organically through use, mirroring the player's actions and choices." "As Henry engages in related activities, these skills level up, unlocking Perks at certain thresholds. Some skills, such as Reading and Pickpocketing, require initial training before they can be improved through practice. Each skill can be levelled up to a maximum of 20." (wiki-only) — [Skills — Kingdom Come: Deliverance Wiki (Fandom)](https://kingdom-come-deliverance.fandom.com/wiki/Skills); "Abilities and stats grow depending on what the player does and says". — [Kingdom Come: Deliverance — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_Come:_Deliverance)
- Skyrim: 18 skills in three schools, each with "its own experience bar that increases when performing related actions"; when full "the skill levels up and the bar is reset. After enough skills level up, the character levels up … providing a perk point". IGN's Charles Onyett: removing classes "allowed players to experiment with different skills without having to make decisions about a class early in the game". — [The Elder Scrolls V: Skyrim — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Elder_Scrolls_V:_Skyrim)
- Dying Light: Power points from "Engaging in combat", Agility points from "performing parkour movement", Survival points from "Completing missions, challenges, and quests"; parkour uses "Natural Movement", which "automatically determine[s] what type of object the player is climbing", built with parkour pioneer David Belle as consultant. — [Dying Light — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dying_Light); skill trees are about order, not exclusion: "The player does not have to choose between two skills, but rather in what order"; Legend skills unlock once a tree is maxed. (wiki-only) — [Skills — Dying Light Wiki (Fandom)](https://dyinglight.fandom.com/wiki/Skills)
- Dying Light 2 (snippet): "the more complex and skillful movements players perform, the more experience will be gained" toward a parkour bar. — [Dying Light 2 skill trees detailed by Techland — Stevivor, 2019](https://stevivor.com/news/dying-light-2-details-new-skill-trees/)
- Crackdown's use-based hybrid: orb type depends on how you dispatched the foe; Agility also from "killing an enemy from a high altitude" and rooftop races. — [Crackdown — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crackdown_(video_game)); orbs on rooftops show a "recommended level" and "levelled-agility orbs will reward the player with more experience". (wiki-only) — [Agility Orb — Crackdown Wiki](https://crackdown.fandom.com/wiki/Agility_Orb)
- Point/story-gated model: Control awards ability points for missions, with Levitate story-locked to mission six and Ground Slam behind Levitate level 3 (see earlier section). inFamous's rule that a new power must be usable "immediately after they got it". — [Infamous — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infamous_(video_game))
- Reputation-capped growth: Gravity Rush caps upgrade level (4 → 5 → 8) by public reputation. (wiki-only) — [Upgrades — Gravity Rush Wiki](https://gravityrush.fandom.com/wiki/Upgrades)

### Inferences
- For "slowly developing" superhuman powers, the use-based model gives the most natural pacing (you jump higher because you have been jumping), but it needs Kingdom Come's "initial training" gate or Gravity Rush's external cap to stop a player from grinding a single stat far past the consequence ladder. Crackdown's "kill from height → Agility" is the precedent for growing a stat *through the risky use of that stat*, which is exactly the brief's consequence coupling.
- Dying Light's "order, not exclusion" and Skyrim's classless perks suggest the consequence-taming powers should be sequenced (always eventually available) rather than mutually exclusive, or the deadlock rule from Dormans is violated.

### Gaps
- No primary Warhorse (Vávra) or Bethesda (Howard) statement on *why* learn-by-doing was chosen was found in opened sources; Wikipedia articles omit it.
- Numeric XP-per-action curves for Kingdom Come or Skyrim were not retrieved.

## Coupling new powers with new controls or UI: teaching a consequence before it kills the player

### Takeaway
Shipped games teach a dangerous verb with (a) a diegetic, context-only indicator (BotW's wheel appears only during effort; Death Stranding's colour-changing tape; Hulk's response meter), (b) an environmental highlight of what the verb applies to (Mirror's Edge Runner Vision, Dying Light Survivor Sense, Crackdown's level-labelled orbs), (c) a proprioceptive telegraph (Titanfall's camera tilt, Dark Souls' longer roll), and (d) a forced, safe first use (inFamous core relays, Titanfall's Gauntlet and enforced wall-runs, Anthropy & Clark's "safe areas").

### Cited Findings
- Runner Vision "turns certain objects to a bold red color as Faith approaches them, allowing the player to instantly recognize paths and escape routes"; senior producer Owen O'Brien said the style "grew out of the gameplay, as it allows the player to focus on the Runner Vision guidance"; HUD is only "a small reticle in the center of the screen", added "to lessen the simulation sickness"; DICE created "hundreds of first-person animations" as "essential to help orient players". — [Mirror's Edge — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mirror%27s_Edge); in Catalyst the system "can recalculate the path for players towards their objectives" (snippet). — [Mirror's Edge Catalyst — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mirror's_Edge_Catalyst)
- BotW stamina UI appears only when needed: "a pie-shaped indicator that appears near Link whenever he is performing an action that requires great effort". (wiki-only) — [Stamina Wheel — Zelda Wiki](https://zelda.fandom.com/wiki/Stamina_Wheel)
- Dying Light Survivor Sense: used "to identify all nearby loot" and "to locate and avoid the infected" at night. — [Dying Light — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dying_Light)
- Telegraphing before commitment: camera tilt "as players jump toward walls but before running begins", plus training sections and enforced sequences. — [Game Developer, 2017](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/designer-interview-getting-i-titanfall-i-s-controls-just-right)
- Forced safe first use: inFamous Second Son core relays ("must destroy D.U.P. Core Relays to learn the basic abilities") — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infamous_Second_Son); inFamous rule of immediate use — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infamous_(video_game)); Gravity Rush's first upgrade "is mandatory in the tutorial mission" (wiki-only) — [Gravity Rush Wiki](https://gravityrush.fandom.com/wiki/Upgrades); Anthropy & Clark's "safe areas … without being placed in immediate danger" — [First Person Scholar, 2014](https://www.firstpersonscholar.com/a-game-design-vocabulary/)
- Diegetic damage readouts: Death Stranding's sensor tape "changes color based on the cargo's integrity—from yellow to red" (wiki-only) — [Death Stranding Wiki](https://deathstranding.fandom.com/wiki/Cargo); Hulk's "emergency response meter" spokes extend with destruction — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Incredible_Hulk:_Ultimate_Destruction); Superman Returns' city damage meter — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Superman_Returns_(video_game))
- Collectible placement as a curriculum: orbs display "the recommended level to collect them at". (wiki-only) — [Agility Orb — Crackdown Wiki](https://crackdown.fandom.com/wiki/Agility_Orb)
- Animation length as the feedback channel: fat roll 46–48 frames vs fast roll 21–24 with identical i-frames. — [Fextralife](https://darksouls.wiki.fextralife.com/Equip_Load)
- Design-theory backing: "clear and immediate feedback" (Csikszentmihalyi) — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flow_(psychology)); Meier: err toward "too much information" and never "just move on" after a decision — [Game Developer, 2012](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/gdc-2012-sid-meier-on-how-to-see-games-as-sets-of-interesting-decisions); Schell: indirect control via "constraints, goals, interface design, and visual guidance" — [notesbylex](https://notesbylex.com/the-art-of-game-design-a-book-of-lenses-2nd-edition-by-jesse-schell)

### Inferences
- A reproducible "consequence tutorial" per tier: (1) show the new limit as a contextual gauge (a kinetic-energy or impact-speed arc that appears only above the safe threshold, in the BotW/Death Stranding style); (2) highlight what tames it (ledges, soft landings, brake surfaces) in the Runner Vision style, and let the player disable the highlight as skill grows; (3) telegraph commitment with camera/body lean before impact (Titanfall); (4) force a first use in a safe scene (core relay / Gauntlet) before the first lethal instance.
- Because DICE found stopping inertia "irritating" even with Runner Vision, the telegraph and highlight should be stronger the higher the tier, not weaker.

### Gaps
- No Techland interview on Survivor Sense design was retrieved; EA's Catalyst Runner's Vision design post returned 404.

## Candidate ability ladders (proposed as findings; each step traced to a source)

### Takeaway
Three ladders are proposed — Vertical (jump/fall), Horizontal (speed/impact) and Strength (load/world) — each 6 tiers to match the Crackdown 3 / Saints Row IV depth, ordered "raw capability, then a self-rescue verb, then tolerance, then mastery", and fed by a Crackdown-style economy (100–150 collectibles per tier, over-supplied by 15–20%) or by use-based XP where the stat grows through its own risky use. Multipliers follow the +5 ft/tier linear step documented for Crackdown and the 75%→50%→0% cost steps documented for Saints Row IV.

### Cited Findings
- Ladder depth and economy anchors: 4 tiers/500 orbs (Crackdown), 6 levels/750 orbs (Crackdown 3), 1,255 clusters with 1,065 needed (Saints Row IV) — [Crackdown Wiki: Skills](https://crackdown.fandom.com/wiki/Skills), [Agility Orb](https://crackdown.fandom.com/wiki/Agility_Orb), [Saints Row Wiki: Clusters](https://saintsrow.fandom.com/wiki/Clusters) (all wiki-only).
- Linear per-tier step: "+5 feet each level", 15→30 ft (Crackdown) and 10→30 ft (Crackdown 2) (wiki-only) — [Crackdown Wiki: Agility](https://crackdown.fandom.com/wiki/Agility).
- Cost-reduction steps 75%→50%→unlimited and exposed parameters (`super_lateral_velocity` 130%, `glide_terminal_velocity` 75%, `super_sprint_buildup_time` 50%) (wiki-only) — [Saints Row Wiki](https://saintsrow.fandom.com/wiki/Super_Powers_in_Saints_Row_IV).
- Prerequisite chains: Airdash → Glide → Airdash Boost; Air Recovery separate — [Altered Gamer](https://www.alteredgamer.com/prototype/39802-powers-movement-and-survivability-guide/); Boosters (L1) → Air Dash (L2) → Double Dash (L4) → Triple Jump (L5); Ground Pound (Str L2) before Resilience (Str L4) — [Shacknews, 2019](https://www.shacknews.com/article/109880/skills-abilities-and-leveling-fast-in-crackdown-3).
- Self-rescue verbs with real consequence: Blink-to-ledge cancels fall damage (wiki-only) — [Dishonored Wiki](https://dishonored.fandom.com/wiki/Blink); grapple/parachute to pull up when the wingsuit reaches the ground — [Just Cause 3 — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Just_Cause_3); JC4 "kick himself away" from an imminent crash (wiki-only) — [Just Cause Wiki](https://justcause.fandom.com/wiki/Wingsuit).
- Load/balance consequence and gauge: Death Stranding weight "can throw off his balance" (wiki-only) — [Death Stranding Wiki](https://deathstranding.fandom.com/wiki/Cargo); Dark Souls 25/50/100% thresholds — [Fextralife](https://darksouls.wiki.fextralife.com/Equip_Load).
- World reaction: ground "visibly cracks" at max agility (snippet) — [Shacknews review](https://www.shacknews.com/article/45712/crackdown-review); Hulk's response meter — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Incredible_Hulk:_Ultimate_Destruction); Superman Returns' city meter — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Superman_Returns_(video_game)).
- Teaching: safe areas — [First Person Scholar](https://www.firstpersonscholar.com/a-game-design-vocabulary/); immediate use — [Infamous — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infamous_(video_game)); camera telegraph — [Game Developer, 2017](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/designer-interview-getting-i-titanfall-i-s-controls-just-right).
- Physics-failure feel: one tightly controlled anchor + floppy body + helper forces — [Octodad postmortem, 2015](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/octodad-dadliest-catch-post-mortem-pt-3-design); Euphoria self-preservation as the reaction layer — [Game Developer, 2007](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/game-platforms/product-i-grand-theft-auto-iv-i-using-naturalmotion-s-euphoria).

### Inferences (the proposed ladders)

**Ladder V — Vertical (jump height vs. landing)**
- V0 Baseline: human jump; ledge grab available from the start (the always-available self-rescue verb, per Dishonored's Blink-to-ledge and Human Fall Flat's grab). Fall damage lethal above a human threshold.
- V1 Jump +1 step (Crackdown's linear +5 ft/tier model; suggested as a fixed additive increment per tier rather than a multiplier). No new landing tolerance → the only survivable way down from a V1 jump onto hard ground is a ledge grab or a soft surface (BotW: stamina runs out → fall; Anthropy & Clark: introduce in a safe area with water/soft debris).
- V2 Air control (Crackdown 3 L2 Air Dash; Prototype Airdash first at 2,500 EP): steer toward ledges; still no tolerance.
- V3 Landing roll / impact tolerance step 1 (Prototype "Air Recovery" as a discrete recovery purchase; Dark Souls roll-length feedback; Euphoria protective fall). Tolerance rises one tier *behind* jump height, so each new height is dangerous for exactly one tier.
- V4 Jump +2 with glide/air-brake (Prototype Glide gated on Airdash; Saints Row IV Glide "Requires Air Dash"; Crackdown 2 wingsuit at the top agility tier).
- V5 Ground pound / Death From Above (Crackdown 3 Ground Pound at Strength L2; Saints Row IV DFA): falls become attacks; the world reacts (cracked ground, per Crackdown; response meter, per Hulk).
- V6 Mastery: sustained boost / "Escape Velocity" (Crackdown 3 L6); tolerance now covers all heights, as in Prototype's no-fall-damage end state.

**Ladder H — Horizontal (sprint speed vs. stopping and impact)**
- H0 Baseline sprint with stamina (BotW wheel; Dark Souls stamina); wall impact at sprint speed staggers via Euphoria, not lethal.
- H1 Speed tier 1 (Prototype Sprint Speed 1 at 10,000 EP; Saints Row IV "Speed" 15 clusters). Impact at this speed is lethal; the only brakes are friction, a ledge/pole grab, or a deliberate ragdoll (Just Cause 3: "crash into something and ragdoll, or switch to Parachute").
- H2 Self-arrest (Titanfall slide; Just Cause 4 "kick himself away"/air brake): a slide-to-stop and wall-kick that costs stamina; telegraphed by camera lean before impact (Titanfall camera tilt).
- H3 Auto-vault (Prototype auto-hop over cars when sprinting; Dying Light Natural Movement) so obstacles stop killing momentum; Speed tier 2.
- H4 Wall run (Saints Row IV Wall Running at rank 6; Titanfall wall-run with speed reward); still impact-lethal head-on.
- H5 Impact tolerance / "Immovable Object" (Saints Row IV top tier "knocks them over but you can't be knocked over"; Crackdown 3 Strength L4 Resilience). Note Saints Row IV front-loads 90% sprint damage reduction at rank 1 — the brief's ordering deliberately inverts this.
- H6 Speed max with stamina cost removed (Saints Row IV 75%→50%→unlimited steps; Prototype Sprint Speed Max 100,000 EP).

**Ladder S — Strength (load vs. balance vs. world reaction)**
- S0 Grab/lift small objects (Crackdown 3 Strength L0 Grab; Human Fall Flat grab).
- S1 Lift heavier (Crackdown 3 L1 Energy Grip); carried load now shifts balance and roll/recovery time (Death Stranding weight → balance; Dark Souls 25/50% thresholds).
- S2 Throw / break walls (Hulk UD weaponised objects; Crackdown Strength via thrown objects); collateral raises a world-response meter (Hulk emergency response; Superman Returns city meter).
- S3 Ground pound and charge (Crackdown 3 Ground Pound L2, Flying Fist L3): self-inflicted knockback/ragdoll until —
- S4 Resilience (Crackdown 3 Strength L4): impact tolerance for strength moves.
- S5–S6 Range/graviton tiers (Crackdown 3 L5–L6) with permanent world-state changes.

**Economy and pacing proposal (traced):** ~100–150 pickups per tier with 15–20% surplus (Crackdown 500/4 and "4 stars before you get them all"; Saints Row IV 190 surplus), pickups labelled with a recommended tier (Crackdown Agility Orb), and/or use-based growth where the stat grows through its own risky use (Crackdown "kill from height → Agility"; Dying Light 2 "more complex and skillful movements … more experience"), capped by a reputation-style external gate (Gravity Rush) so a tolerance tier cannot lag more than one behind its capability tier (Dormans' deadlock rule).

### Gaps
- No shipped game was found that both (a) grows movement powers gradually and (b) keeps lethal physics consequences throughout; the ladders combine precedents rather than copy one. Playtest validation is required, especially given DICE's finding that inertia felt "irritating".
- Exact per-tier speed/jump multipliers in the shipped games (m/s, metres) are not documented outside wikis; the only engine-facing numbers found are Saints Row IV's exposed parameters (130%, 75%, 50%) and Just Cause 3's player-measured 55/75 m/s.
