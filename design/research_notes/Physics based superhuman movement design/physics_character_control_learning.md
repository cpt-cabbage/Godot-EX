# Learning-based and data-driven physics character control (2016–2026): survey and feasibility for a Godot 4.8-dev / Jolt first-person game

Research date: 2026-09-27. Method note: arXiv PDFs came back as binary through WebFetch, so every paper below was downloaded with curl and its text extracted locally (PyMuPDF) and read from the primary PDF. The session's web-search budget was exhausted about two-thirds of the way through; the last third of this work used direct fetches of known URLs only, which is why a few items (Sony, EA SEED talks, Embark primary sources) end in explicit gaps rather than findings. ## Key question 1 — What are the two user-supplied sources, and what in them is reusable?

### Takeaway
Neither user-supplied source is about physics-based or learned character control. arXiv 2105.09153 is a 2021 draft review of procedural animation in interactive art installations (projection mapping, VR/AR exhibits); HAL inria-00516890 is a 2003 CASA paper on real-time procedural grass/tree animation with a "tread on grass" primitive driven by walking characters. The only reusable ideas for this project are the "environment reacts to the character's motion" primitive (footprints/crushed grass as cheap procedural feedback of momentum) and a simple perception→decision→motion-controller decomposition; nothing in either source can be cited for controller design, ragdoll physics, or learning.

### Cited Findings
- arXiv 2105.09153 is "Procedural animations in interactive art experiences – A state of the art review" by C. Tollola (University of Ottawa), submitted 16 May 2021, category cs.HC, DOI 10.48550/arXiv.2105.09153; the PDF header is marked "DRAFT" — [arXiv abstract page](https://arxiv.org/abs/2105.09153); [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2105.09153)
- Tollola's abstract: the review "broadly oversees the use of novel research utilized in the creation of virtual environments applied in interactive art experiences, with a specific focus on the application of procedural animation in spatially augmented reality (SAR) exhibitions", covering VR, AR, MR and robotics for museums, tours, concerts and theatre over "the last three years" — [Tollola 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2105.09153)
- Tollola defines procedural animation as "the generation of rules-based motion generation, stemming from inverse and forward kinematics", notes these rules "generally apply to robotics control", and names Unity and Maya as tools; the section list is: case studies and emerging applications; projection mapping; hardware; possible artistic effects and animations — [Tollola 2021, §2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2105.09153)
- The only character-animation content in Tollola is (a) a smartphone "virtual puppeteering" app whose characters are rigged in Unity and driven by a state machine whose state is "defined by internal logic, the root position of the character, the configuration of the animation, and the inverse kinematic state" (Fig. 4), and (b) one sentence citing Holden, Komura and Saito's Phase-Functioned Neural Networks (2017) as neural rendering that lets "a character in a video game to be automatically properly animated, for example, to run over complex terrain" — [Tollola 2021, §3.2 and §6.2, ref. 95](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2105.09153)
- Tollola contains no discussion of physics simulation, ragdolls, PD control, motion matching, reinforcement learning or any controller; its concrete numbers are about displays (e.g., touch-feedback delay "greater than 6.04ms" disrupts immersion; a 1000 Hz procam; face projection mapping latency 10 ms; TeamLab Borderless uses "520 computers, 470 Epson projectors") — [Tollola 2021, §4.4–4.6](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2105.09153)
- HAL inria-00516890 is "A Procedural Approach to Animate Interactive Natural Sceneries" by Sylvain Guerraz, Frank Perbet, David Raulo, François Faure and Marie-Paule Cani (GRAVIR/IMAG), 16th International Conference on Computer Animation and Social Agents (CASA 2003), May 2003, New Brunswick, NJ — [HAL record via HAL API](https://api.archives-ouvertes.fr/search/?q=halId_s:inria-00516890&fl=title_s,authFullName_s,producedDateY_i,conferenceTitle_s,abstract_s,files_s,uri_s&wt=json); canonical record [https://inria.hal.science/inria-00516890v1](https://inria.hal.science/inria-00516890v1)
- Guerraz et al. abstract: the method "improves the prairie model of [11] by enabling the on-the-fly generation of blades of grass, at three different levels of detail, from user-specified density maps. A new animation function, the tread on grass, is defined to allow virtual objects or characters to crush the grass", with trees responding to the same wind and "real-time animation of autonomous virtual humans interacting with this natural scenery" — [Guerraz et al. 2003, PDF mirror](http://evasion.imag.fr/Publications/2003/GPRFC03/paper.pdf)
- The treading primitive: at each time step a segment from the object's previous to current position generates a scalar field with finite radius using the closed-form convolution field F(P) = (sin α1 − sin α2)/distance²(P,H); the displacement vector is E(P) = F(P)(sign(A·N)·N + C), "tuned such that a maximally crushed blade is almost horizontal"; each node stores the crush time and interpolates back to zero over a given interval so blades "progressively recover their uncrunched shape" — [Guerraz et al. 2003, §4.2](http://evasion.imag.fr/Publications/2003/GPRFC03/paper.pdf)
- The characters are kinematic, not physical: each is "controlled through a behaviour model composed of three basic components: (1) a system for simulating perception, (2) a decisional process for navigation, (3) a real-time motion controller"; perception uses GJK shortest distance to the nearest obstacle (d, θ); the decision maps (d, θ) to (v, φ) with Gaussian noise; the gait "is based on key-frames obtained from cyclic motion data of a human character walking … interpolated using Bezier-curves", with "the character current velocity … used to adapt the motion amplitude of the skeleton" — [Guerraz et al. 2003, §5](http://evasion.imag.fr/Publications/2003/GPRFC03/paper.pdf)
- The paper explicitly frames itself for games: "the user should not only be able to visit this animated scenery, but also to interact with it … feeling the effect of the local slope and vegetation onto the avatar's motion and seeing the immediate consequences of this motion on the scenery, such as foot-prints or grass trodden down"; sponsored "by Infograme and … the CNC … through the PRIAM program" — [Guerraz et al. 2003, §1 and acknowledgments](http://evasion.imag.fr/Publications/2003/GPRFC03/paper.pdf)
- The grass's 2.5D LOD "is only adapted to grazing view angles, which is reasonable in the case of a walker's point of view" — a stated fit with a first-person camera — [Guerraz et al. 2003, §3.4](http://evasion.imag.fr/Publications/2003/GPRFC03/paper.pdf)

### Inferences
- The two sources were probably supplied as general "procedural animation" background rather than as controller references; for a design document that must be reproducible from citations, they can support only (a) the idea that the environment should visibly record the character's momentum (crushed grass, footprints, later: dents in walls) and (b) the classic perception/decision/motion-controller split. Controller claims must be sourced from the papers in Key Question 2.
- Guerraz's "tread" convolution field is directly reusable as a cheap, artist-tunable way to make superhuman sprinting and landings leave a trail without simulating vegetation physically; the recover-over-time interpolation maps naturally onto "impact leaves a mark that fades".

## Key question 2 — Survey of learned physics-based character controllers: formulations, training setups, runtime costs, known limitations

### Takeaway
Since DeepMimic (2018) the field has converged on one runtime recipe: a neural policy queried at 30 Hz (20–40 Hz in some works) outputs PD-controller targets for every joint of a ragdoll simulated at 60–1200 Hz; what differs is how the policy is trained (tracking reward, adversarial motion prior, latent skill space, supervised world model, masked inpainting, behavior foundation model) and how much compute that takes — from ~30 h on 8–16 CPU cores (DeepMimic, DReCon) to ~2 weeks on 4×A100 (MaskedMimic) and 21,000 GPU-hours (SONIC 2026). Inference is cheap (DReCon: 60 µs per frame for a 2×128 MLP; SuperTrack: ~1.8 ms for a 5×1024 MLP), but every paper reports residual artifacts (jitter, foot sliding, mode collapse, failures on backflips/uneven terrain) and none was trained in a game engine's physics except DReCon (Bullet) and SuperTrack (PhysX/Havok/Bullet).

### Cited Findings

DeepMimic (Peng, Abbeel, Levine, van de Panne, ACM TOG 37(4)/SIGGRAPH 2018)
- Formulation: RL policy imitating a reference clip; "Each action specifies target angles for proportional-derivative (PD) controllers that then produce the final torques"; "The policy is queried at 30Hz"; the paper argues PD targets "abstract away low-level control details such as local damping and local feedback" compared to torques — [DeepMimic PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1804.02717); [ACM DOI 10.1145/3197517.3201311](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3197517.3201311)
- Network: "two fully-connected layers with 1024, and 512 units each, followed by a linear output layer. ReLU activations"; heightmap tasks add 3 conv layers (16 8×8, 32 4×4, 32 4×4 filters) and a 64-unit FC layer — [DeepMimic §Network](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1804.02717)
- Simulation: "Physics simulation is performed at 1.2kHz using the Bullet physics engine … The characters' motions are driven by torques computed using stable PD controllers [Tan et al. 2011]" — [DeepMimic §10](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1804.02717)
- Training cost: policies "typically require about 60 million samples to train, requiring about 2 days on an 8-core machine. All simulation and network updates are performed on the CPU and no GPU acceleration is used" — [DeepMimic appendix](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1804.02717)
- Two ingredients the paper calls "two of the most important components": reference state initialization (RSI) and early termination (ET) on torso/head ground contact; "For the backflip, without RSI, the policy never learns to perform a full mid-air flip" — [DeepMimic §10.4](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1804.02717)
- Robustness (Table 6): pushes applied to the pelvis for 0.2 s, increased by 10 N until the character falls; maximum tolerated forward/sideways: Backflip 440/100 N, Cartwheel 200/470 N, Run 720/300 N, Spin-kick 690/600 N, Walk 240/300 N; "no external perturbations are applied during the training process; we suspect that the policies' robustness is in large part due to the exploration noise" — [DeepMimic §10.5](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1804.02717)
- Limitation: training "is also quite time consuming, often requiring several days per skill" — [DeepMimic §11](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1804.02717)

AMP (Peng, Ma, Abbeel, Levine, Kanazawa, ACM TOG 40(4)/SIGGRAPH 2021)
- Formulation: adversarial motion prior — a discriminator trained to score dataset transitions 1 and policy transitions −1 provides a style reward, combined with a task reward, so no clip synchronization or phase is required; gradient-penalty coefficient w_gp = 10 — [AMP PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2104.02180)
- Setup: "All environments are simulated using the Bullet physics engine … with a simulation frequency of 1.2kHz. The policy is queried at 30Hz, and each action specifies target positions for PD controllers"; policy network "two hidden layers, consisting of 1024 and 512" units — [AMP §8.1](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2104.02180)
- Training cost: "each policy is trained with 100-300 million samples, requiring approximately 30-140 hours on 16 CPU cores" — [AMP §8.1](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2104.02180)
- Fall handling: adding get-up clips to the dataset "enable[s] our character to recover from a fall", and the policy "discovers novel recovery behaviors that are not present in the dataset. When the character falls forward, it tucks its body into a roll during the fall in order to more quickly transition into a getup behavior" — [AMP §8](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2104.02180)
- Limitations: "like many other GAN-based techniques, AMP is susceptible to mode collapse. When provided with a large dataset of diverse motion clips, the policy is prone to imitating only a small subset"; priors are "trained from scratch for each policy" — [AMP §Discussion](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2104.02180)

ASE (Peng, Guo, Halper, Levine, Fidler, ACM TOG 41(4)/SIGGRAPH 2022)
- Formulation: a low-level skill-conditioned policy π(a|s,z) trained with an AMP-style discriminator plus a skill-discovery (mutual-information) objective over a 64-D hyperspherical latent; a high-level policy ω(z|s,g) is trained per task on top — [ASE PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2205.01906)
- Setup and cost: "All environments are simulated using Isaac Gym … 4096 environments are simulated in parallel on a single NVIDIA V100 GPU, with a simulation frequency of 120Hz. The low-level policy operates at 30Hz, while the high-level policy operates at 6Hz"; "The low-level policy is trained with over 10 billion samples … 10 days on a single GPU"; low-level network "3 hidden layers of [1024, 1024, 512] units" — [ASE §Experimental setup](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2205.01906)
- Recovery: "the character is also trained to recover from a large variety of fallen configurations … initialized in a random fallen state at the start of each episode. The fallen states are generated by dropping the character from the air" so that "the character … automatically recover[s] from perturbations when performing downstream tasks, without needing to be explicitly trained to recover from falling for each new task" — [ASE §Robust recovery strategies](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2205.01906)
- Limitations: "highly sample intensive"; "the low-level policy can still occasionally produce unnatural motions, such as jittering, sudden jerks, and overly energetic recovery behaviors" — [ASE §Discussion](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2205.01906)

CALM (Tessler, Kasten, Guo, Mannor, Chechik, Peng, SIGGRAPH 2023)
- Formulation: conditional adversarial latent model — a motion encoder maps clips to a 64-D hypersphere latent; a latent-conditioned discriminator and low-level policy are trained jointly, then a high-level policy or a hand-authored FSM selects latents — [CALM PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2305.02195)
- Setup and cost: "We parallelize training over 4096 Isaac Gym environments on a single A100 GPU, for a total of 5 billion steps. The low-level (high-level) policy takes decisions at a rate of 30 (6) Hz" — [CALM §Experiments](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2305.02195)
- Limitations named by the authors: mode collapse remains ("controllability … from 35% up to 78%" vs ASE); unseen motions unsupported; "the policy's robustness envelope may limit its ability to solve tasks with vastly different dynamics from those seen during training, such as climbing stairs or walking on uneven terrain"; rendering the physics character with a different visual mesh causes penetrations — [CALM §Limitations](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2305.02195)

PHC — Perpetual Humanoid Control (Luo, Cao, Winkler, Kitani, Xu, ICCV 2023)
- Formulation: progressive multiplicative control policy — primitives trained progressively on harder motions, frozen, then a composer; a dedicated fail-state-recovery primitive; each primitive/discriminator/value net is a "two-layer multilayer perceptron (MLP) with dimensions [1024, 512]"; three fail states are defined: "1) fallen on the ground; 2) far-away from the reference; 3) fallen and faraway" — [PHC PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2305.06456)
- Results and cost: "fully imitate 98.9% of the AMASS dataset" (11,313 training sequences); "PHC can be trained on a single NVIDIA A100 GPU; it takes around a week to train all primitives and the composer. Once trained, the composite policy runs at > 30 FPS. Physics simulation is carried out in NVIDIA's Isaac Gym. The control policy is run at 30 Hz, while simulation runs at 60 Hz" — [PHC §Implementation](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2305.06456)
- Limitations: "can still struggle to perform more dynamic motions, such as backflipping"; in live use "a noticeable degradation in performance from the offline counterparts" caused by noisy/jittery 3D keypoints, velocity estimation, and frame-rate mismatch (PHC "assumes 30 FPS motion input") — [PHC Appendix D](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2305.06456)

PULSE (Luo, Cao, Merel, Winkler, Huang, Kitani, Xu, ICLR 2024)
- Formulation: distill an improved PHC+ into a 32-D latent action space via a variational information bottleneck (encoder, decoder, learnable prior conditioned on proprioception); downstream tasks then act in latent space — [PULSE PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2310.04582)
- Numbers: PHC+ "use[s] only three primitives to learn fail-state recovery and achieve a success rate of 100%" on AMASS train; "Simulation is conducted at Isaac Gym, where the policy is run at 30 Hz and the simulation at 60 Hz … each policy or primitive is a 6-layer MLP. Our encoder E, decoder D, prior R, CALM, and ASE are three-layer MLPs. The latent space is 32d … about half of the humanoid DOF of 69"; latent-space baselines were trained "with around 1 billion samples" — [PULSE §Experiments](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2310.04582)
- Design note relevant to control rates: "Both CALM and ASE use a 64-d latent code … designed to be multi-frame latent codes operating at 6 HZ. Since our policy produces a per-frame latent code (30 Hz)…" — [PULSE §Baselines](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2310.04582)

MaskedMimic (Tessler, Guo, Nabati, Chechik, Peng, ACM TOG / SIGGRAPH Asia 2024)
- Formulation: physics-based control as "a general motion inpainting problem": a fully-constrained tracker π_FC (transformer over tokenized inputs) is distilled into a partially-constrained conditional-VAE π_PC whose prior observes masked goals (any-joint-any-time keyframes, text via XCLIP, objects, terrain); "the encoder is used solely for training, and is not utilized at runtime" — [MaskedMimic PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2409.14393); [ACM DOI 10.1145/3687951](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3687951)
- Setup and cost: "All physics simulation are performed using Isaac Gym, each with 16,384 parallel environments, split across 4 A100 GPUs. Models are trained for approximately 2 weeks"; "controllers operate at 30 Hz, and the simulation runs at 120 Hz"; the prior transformer "utilize[s] a latent dimension of 512 whereas the internal feed-forward size is 1024. We use 4 layers and 4 self-attention" heads; encoder MLP hidden layers [1024, 1024, 1024]; 64-D latent — [MaskedMimic appendix](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2409.14393)
- Limitations: "some generated motions exhibit unnatural jittering"; "some challenging motions such as backflips and breakdancing remaining difficult"; "when navigating irregular terrain, the character tends to mimic standard walking motions rather than planning more suitable foot placements" — [MaskedMimic §Limitations](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2409.14393)

ControlVAE (Yao, Song, Chen, Liu, ACM TOG / SIGGRAPH Asia 2022)
- Formulation: model-based learning of a conditional-VAE generative policy with a state-conditioned prior, trained through a learned differentiable world model ω(s_{t+1}|s_t,a_t); 64-D latent; downstream tasks solved by RL or sampling-based MPC in latent space — [ControlVAE PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2210.06063)
- Setup: "PD control parameters (kp,kd) = (400, 50) … for all the joints, except for the toe joints (10, 1) and the wrist joints (5, 1). The character is simulated using the Open Dynamics Engine (ODE) at 120 Hz. The stable-PD mechanism … is implemented"; "Our system executes ControlVAE, the world model, and all the task-specific policies at 20 Hz"; "Once trained, the entire system runs faster than real-time on a desktop" — [ControlVAE §5.1](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2210.06063)
- Cost: "Our full training takes 20,000 iterations and about 50 hours with four parallel working threads on an Intel Xeon Gold 6240 @ 2.60GHz CPU and one NVIDIA GTX 2080Ti"; dataset 3.3 hours after mirroring — [ControlVAE §5.2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2210.06063)
- Behaviors: the character "is also capable of resisting external perturbation or recovering from tumbling when moving" (Fig. 10f–g) — [ControlVAE §5.3](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2210.06063)

Motion VAEs (Ling, Zinno, Cheng, van de Panne, ACM TOG 39(4)/SIGGRAPH 2020) — kinematic, included because the user listed it
- "we learn a kinematic generative model of human motion"; "Our work focuses on kinematics motion synthesis" — it does not simulate physics — [MVAE PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2103.14274)
- Data and cost: "17 minutes of walking, running … 30 Hz and contains about 30,000 frames"; encoder "three-layer feed-forward … 256 hidden units … ELU"; decoder is a mixture of six experts; MVAE training "takes roughly two hours on an Nvidia GeForce GTX 1060 and an Intel i7-5960X CPU"; RL controllers on top take up to "six hours on our desktop machine" — [MVAE §Implementation](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2103.14274)

DReCon (Bergamin, Clavet, Holden, Forbes — McGill / Ubisoft La Forge, ACM TOG 38(6)/SIGGRAPH Asia 2019)
- Formulation: Motion Matching produces a kinematic target from a 10-minute mocap database; an RL policy outputs "corrective changes to the PD control targets" so the ragdoll follows the kinematic character; state 110-D, action 25-D; "2-layer fully connected neural network … 128 hidden units … tanh" — [DReCon PDF](https://static-wordpress.ubisoft.com/montreal.ubisoft.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/11/13214229/DReCon.pdf); [Ubisoft La Forge page](https://www.ubisoft.com/en-us/studio/laforge/news/VjEIwquaIyEZZSw5RZI0V/drecon-datadriven-responsive-control-of-physicsbased-characters)
- Game-engine-like physics: "A simulation environment is created in C++ using the Bullet physics engine … Velocity based constraints are used to simulate PD servo motors at the joints, with motor constraint torques clamped to 200 Nm … A simulation frequency of 60 Hz is used, with 32 constraint solver iterations per simulation step"; policy queried at 30 Hz — [DReCon §6.1, Table 2](https://static-wordpress.ubisoft.com/montreal.ubisoft.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/11/13214229/DReCon.pdf)
- Cost: PPO (OpenAI baselines), "8 simulations in parallel, and achieve an average of 2500 environment steps per second on a single machine"; plateau "after training for around 3 × 10^8 steps (around 30 hours)" — [DReCon §6.2, §7](https://static-wordpress.ubisoft.com/montreal.ubisoft.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/11/13214229/DReCon.pdf)
- Runtime (Table 4, single-threaded Intel Xeon E5-1650 v3): Kinematic control 40 µs, State construction 40 µs, Policy evaluation 60 µs, Physics simulation 200 µs, Total 340 µs per frame — "within the typical budget for character animation and physics provided by a AAA video-game" — [DReCon §7.6](https://static-wordpress.ubisoft.com/montreal.ubisoft.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/11/13214229/DReCon.pdf)
- Robustness: impact test running at 3 m/s with projectiles; "Robustness is demonstrated even if the projectiles are not used during training. Training with projectiles significantly increases robustness, without visibly impacting the resulting motion style" — [DReCon §7.1, Fig. 11](https://static-wordpress.ubisoft.com/montreal.ubisoft.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/11/13214229/DReCon.pdf)
- Limitations: "We focused our work on humanoid locomotion on flat terrain"; "our character can sometimes fall when control requirements are too severe, and we have not yet developed good methods of making a character stand up from a fall"; the low sim rate makes "some constraint solver noise … visible" — [DReCon §8](https://static-wordpress.ubisoft.com/montreal.ubisoft.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/11/13214229/DReCon.pdf)

SuperTrack (Fussell, Bergamin, Holden — Ubisoft La Forge, ACM TOG 40(6)/SIGGRAPH Asia 2021)
- Formulation: supervised learning through a learned differentiable world model that predicts the next ragdoll state from state + PD targets; the policy outputs PD-target rotation offsets and is trained by back-propagating a tracking loss through the world model over a window (N_Π = 32 frames); world model and policy are each "5 hidden layers, 1024 units, ELU" — [SuperTrack PDF](https://theorangeduck.com/media/uploads/other_stuff/SuperTrack.pdf); [Ubisoft La Forge page](https://www.ubisoft.com/en-us/studio/laforge/news/7fMzaMaDgnd0gqPsCaJZYb/supertrack-motion-tracking-for-physically-simulated-characters-using-supervised-learning)
- Cost: data "at a rate of ∼5000 samples per second"; "basic balancing behaviour after about ∼10,000 iterations (… about two hours training on a mid-tier graphics card such as a Nvidia GTX 1070) … full training required ∼100,000 to ∼200,000 iterations, and took closer to ∼20 to ∼40 hours"; the Ubisoft summary states 40 hours of training on LaFAN (4.5+ h of mocap) reached 80% success at tracking 30+ s clips vs 5% for PPO under identical conditions, and interactive control from 30 minutes of data in 3 hours — [SuperTrack §3.6](https://theorangeduck.com/media/uploads/other_stuff/SuperTrack.pdf); [Ubisoft page](https://www.ubisoft.com/en-us/studio/laforge/news/7fMzaMaDgnd0gqPsCaJZYb/supertrack-motion-tracking-for-physically-simulated-characters-using-supervised-learning)
- Runtime: "Policy network evaluation is performed every frame, takes ∼1800µs, and requires ∼20MB of storage for the network weights … We run our physics simulations at 240 Hz, with 20 iterations of the constraint solver, which typically takes between 300µs and 1000µs per-frame depending on the simulator used. All performance tests were run on an Intel Xeon E5-1650 CPU" — [SuperTrack §5.3](https://theorangeduck.com/media/uploads/other_stuff/SuperTrack.pdf)
- Cross-engine: results shown in PhysX by default; "we compare results across three different simulators: PhysX, Havok, and Bullet. Since Bullet and Havok do not easily support PD target rotational velocities we set these to zero … our method's final performance is reasonably similar across simulators" — [SuperTrack §5.1.3](https://theorangeduck.com/media/uploads/other_stuff/SuperTrack.pdf)
- Robustness: "Cubes of increasing mass are thrown at the character at a rate of once per second with a speed of 5 m/s"; SuperTrack "produces policies more robust over a range of perturbation sizes" than equal-time PPO — [SuperTrack §5.1.7](https://theorangeduck.com/media/uploads/other_stuff/SuperTrack.pdf)
- Limitations: "it can be more difficult for it to discover novel behaviours such as stepping motions to recover from falls"; "artifacts such as foot sliding can appear"; world model "struggles to maintain the joint constraints" beyond 64 predicted frames; using the world model in place of physics at inference "is often more expensive than the original simulation due to the relatively large network size" — [SuperTrack §Limitations](https://theorangeduck.com/media/uploads/other_stuff/SuperTrack.pdf)

Learned Motion Matching (Holden, Kanoun, Perepichka, Popa — Ubisoft La Forge, ACM TOG 39(4)/SIGGRAPH 2020) — kinematic, included because the user listed it
- LMM replaces the motion-matching database with three networks (Decompressor, Stepper, Projector) to cut memory; "The primary limitation of Motion Matching is that the memory usage (and runtime cost) … scales with the data"; the paper was evaluated "in a AAA production where Motion Matching is used for third-person player locomotion" — [LMM PDF](https://theorangeduck.com/media/uploads/other_stuff/Learned_Motion_Matching.pdf)
- Runtime measured "single-threaded on an Intel Xeon 3.5Ghz 12 Core CPU using a custom, optimized neural network inference library"; small networks made CPU training "almost always more efficient than … the GPU"; blending "∼20µs per frame"; Projector runs "only every N = 10 frames"; Decompressor positional error 1.4 cm ± 1.1 cm — [LMM §6.2](https://theorangeduck.com/media/uploads/other_stuff/Learned_Motion_Matching.pdf)

UniCon (Wang, Guo, Shugrina, Fidler — NVIDIA, arXiv 2020)
- Formulation: torque-based policy (not PD): "we use a torque based controller for UniCon … three hidden layers of 1024 units"; trained with "4096 workers" on the GPU "Flex physics engine" (CUDA Newton PCR solver); a high-level motion scheduler drives the low-level executor from keyboard, video pose or reference motion — [UniCon PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2011.15119)
- Robustness: "our low-level executor exhibits zero-shot robustness to environment perturbations not seen during training" (projectiles thrown at rates from 1/1 Hz to 1/40 Hz in the ablation) and "recovering skills can be learnt automatically without the need to train a separate recovery network" — [UniCon §8.5](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2011.15119)

PhysHOI (Wang, Wang, Zhang, Yuan, Xu, Zhang — 2023)
- Human–object interaction imitation with a contact-graph reward; "the mean is modeled by a two-layer MLP of [1024, 512] units … The simulation and the PD controller run at 60 Hz. The policy is sampled at 30Hz"; "We use Isaac Gym … on a single Nvidia A100 GPU, with 2048 parallel environments"; because "Isaac Gym does not yet provide contact detection APIs for the GPU pipeline, we use force" sensors instead — [PhysHOI PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2312.04393)

PARC (Xu, Shi, Yin, Peng, SIGGRAPH 2025)
- Iterative data augmentation: a terrain-conditioned kinematic motion generator proposes motions on new terrains, a DeepMimic-style physics tracker corrects them, corrected motions are added back; "The policy is queried at 30Hz, while the physics simulation is performed at 120Hz" in Isaac Gym; "the PARC framework is applied for three iterations using a single A6000 GPU, requiring approximately one month to complete" — [PARC PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2505.04002); [ACM DOI 10.1145/3721238.3730616](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3721238.3730616)

Learning to Get Up (Tao, Wilson, Gou, van de Panne, SIGGRAPH 2022)
- Get-up from arbitrary fallen states without mocap using a three-stage curriculum ("Discover" with strong torques, "Weaker" with decreasing torque limits, "Slower" imitation); "All the training tasks are simulated using Mujoco engine running at 800 Hz while all the control policies run at 40 Hz. Our character is roughly 1.5 m tall and weighs 38.3 kg … 21 DoFs"; SAC; each policy trained "on a 32-core CPU desktop with Nvidia Geforce RTX 2070 GPU for roughly 24 hours" — [Get Up PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2205.00307); [project page](https://tianxintao.github.io/get_up_control/)

Meta Motivo / FB-CPR (Tirinzoni et al., Meta FAIR, 2024–2025) and Won et al. 2022 (Meta)
- Motivo is a behavior foundation model for a SMPL humanoid solving tracking, pose-reaching and reward tasks zero-shot; "The simulation is performed using MuJoCo at 450 Hz, while the control frequency is 30 Hz"; scaling "from 25M to 288M parameters" — [Motivo PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2504.11054)
- Motivo limitations: "FB-CPR struggles with problems far from motion-capture datasets, such as tracking motions or solving reward-based tasks involving ground movements … it sometimes generates imperfect and unnatural movements, particularly for behaviors like falling or standing"; "limited to proprioceptive observations and cannot solve tasks requiring environmental navigation or object interaction" — [Motivo §Limitations](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2504.11054)
- "Physics-based character controllers using conditional VAEs" (Won, Gopinath, Hodgins, Meta, SIGGRAPH 2022): task-agnostic cVAE controllers from public mocap that "generate more than a few minutes of motion without conditioning on specific goals" and solve downstream tasks without training from scratch — [Meta Research page](https://research.facebook.com/publications/physics-based-character-controllers-using-conditional-vaes/); ACM record [DOI 10.1145/3528223.3530067](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3528223.3530067)

State of the art 2025–2026
- SONIC (Luo, Yuan, Wang … Fan, Zhu; NVIDIA; Science Robotics 11(117), 2026; arXiv Nov 2025): "scaling model capacity, data, and compute yields a generalist humanoid controller"; models from 1.2M to 42M parameters; 100M+ frames from 700 hours of mocap; 21,000 GPU-hours of training — [arXiv 2511.07820](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.07820); [Science Robotics DOI](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/scirobotics.aed4592)
- BFMTrack (Rupf, Serifi, Müller, Christen, Grandia, Knoop, Bächer; arXiv June 2026): Latent Sequence Optimization lets a behavior foundation model do time-varying motion tracking ("dense motion tracking, sparse keyframing, and real robot deployment") because BFMs otherwise "struggle with sequential motion tasks" — [arXiv 2606.25056](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.25056)
- LYRIC (Han et al., arXiv Sept 2026): flow-matching controller for "contact-rich whole-body object interactions from a free-form language instruction"; tracker success 64.3% vs 53.2% baseline; unified policy 76.5%; held-out 90.3% vs 74.2% — [arXiv 2609.19688](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.19688)
- NVIDIA's ProtoMotions ("a GPU-accelerated simulation and learning framework for training physically simulated digital humans and humanoid robots", Apache-2.0) is the open training stack behind MaskedMimic-style work; ProtoMotions3 targets IsaacSim 5.0+ and text-to-motion via Kimodo — [NVlabs/ProtoMotions](https://github.com/NVlabs/ProtoMotions)
- The 2022 Eurographics STAR on RL in character animation (Kwiatkowski, Alvarado, Kalogeiton, Liu, Pettré, van de Panne, Cani) lists the design decisions every physics-based project must make: "joint torque limits, contact friction, mass distribution, joint limits, joint damping, simulation and control time steps … The choice of action space … joint torques, joint PD-target angles, joint PD-target angle offsets from an available reference motion, muscle-based activations, or more abstract actions" — [Kwiatkowski et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2203.04735)

### Inferences
- Across 15 papers the runtime interface is stable enough to design against today: observation = proprioception (+ goal), action = per-joint PD target (or offset) at 20–30 Hz, physics at 60–240 Hz for game-engine-class solvers (DReCon 60 Hz, SuperTrack 240 Hz) or 1.2 kHz for research setups. A hand-authored controller written to the same interface (PD targets from a kinematic pose plus feedback corrections) is therefore forward-compatible with a learned policy dropped in later.
- The two Ubisoft papers are the only ones that were deliberately engineered for game budgets (60 Hz physics, 2×128 policy, 340 µs/frame; or 5×1024 policy at 1.8 ms). Everything from ASE onward assumes a GPU simulator with 4k–16k parallel environments and days-to-weeks of training that a small team cannot iterate on quickly.
- Fall recovery is now standard in research controllers (ASE, PHC, PULSE, AMP-with-getup-clips, ControlVAE), but the authors themselves report it as jittery or "overly energetic" (ASE) and unnatural for "falling or standing" (Motivo) — the exact moments this game wants to look good.

### Gaps
- UniCon's paper text does not state training wall-clock hours or GPU count; only the 4096-worker count and network size were found.
- I could not open the ACM DL page for Won et al. 2022 (HTTP 403) and used Meta's publication page (abstract only); simulator, rates and robustness tests for that paper are unverified.
- "Physics-Based Motion Imitation with Adversarial Differential Discriminators" (SIGGRAPH Asia 2025), "PDP: Physics-Based Character Animation via Diffusion Policy" (2024) and "HiFAR" humanoid fall recovery (2025) surfaced only as search snippets and were not read; they are listed under search terms below rather than cited as findings.

## Key question 3 — Which methods handle hard landings, pushes, running into walls, catching ledges, and recovering from a fall? Any that model injury or fatigue?

### Takeaway
Pushes and projectile hits are the best-covered event (quantified in DeepMimic, DReCon, SuperTrack, UniCon); fall recovery is covered by ASE/PHC/PULSE/AMP/ControlVAE and, without mocap, by Learning to Get Up; ledge catching appears only as an emergent behavior in PARC (2025) and wall-run-ins only as a failure mode there; no paper models injury as a game mechanic, but Learning to Get Up shows controllers adapting to a "leg and an arm in a cast", and two papers (Cheema et al. 2023; Tired Actor 2026) model cumulative muscle fatigue with the Three-Compartment Controller model — the closest analogue to this game's consequence-gated powers.

### Cited Findings
- Pushes: DeepMimic's run policy survives 720 N × 0.2 s forward and 300 N sideways pelvis pushes; walk survives 240/300 N; spin-kick 690/600 N — [DeepMimic Table 6](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1804.02717)
- Projectiles: DReCon tests impacts while the character "runs at 3 m/s and switches direction every 4 seconds" and finds training with projectiles "significantly increases robustness, without visibly impacting the resulting motion style" — [DReCon §7.1](https://static-wordpress.ubisoft.com/montreal.ubisoft.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/11/13214229/DReCon.pdf); SuperTrack throws "Cubes of increasing mass … once per second with a speed of 5 m/s" — [SuperTrack §5.1.7](https://theorangeduck.com/media/uploads/other_stuff/SuperTrack.pdf); UniCon shows "zero-shot robustness to environment perturbations not seen during training" — [UniCon §8.5](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2011.15119)
- Fall recovery from arbitrary fallen states: ASE trains recovery by "dropping the character from the air" into random fallen states so later tasks inherit it — [ASE](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2205.01906); PHC adds an explicit fail-state-recovery primitive for "fallen on the ground; far-away from the reference; fallen and faraway" and "can rise from fallen state and naturally" return to the reference — [PHC](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2305.06456); PULSE's PHC+ reaches "a success rate of 100%" including fail-state recovery — [PULSE](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2310.04582); AMP learns "novel recovery behaviors … tucks its body into a roll during the fall" — [AMP](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2104.02180); ControlVAE recovers "from tumbling when moving" — [ControlVAE](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2210.06063)
- Fall recovery without mocap and with weakness constraints: Learning to Get Up starts "from a rag-doll fall at 1.5 m above the ground with a randomized pose", finds that "large torque limits are essential to exploration" and then weakens the character with a curriculum until "the final torque limit with curriculum drops to below 60% of the default"; strategies "ending with torque limits below 60% … [are] more natural than those terminating with high torque limits (above 70%)" — [Get Up §5](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2205.00307)
- Injury-like constraints: Learning to Get Up trains variants "with a leg and an arm in a cast and a character with a missing arm. To simulate the character with a leg and an arm in a cast, we lock the left elbow joint and the right knee joint"; the cast character "adopts a get-up strategy that relies on the remaining limbs to gain momentum, while using the limbs in casts for balance" — [Get Up §6](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2205.00307); [project page](https://tianxintao.github.io/get_up_control/)
- Ledge catching (emergent, physics-tracked): PARC's "physically simulated character is able to sequence together different skills such as jumping across a gap and catching onto a ledge"; Fig. 7: "A character first jumps a gap, then holds onto a ledge and drops. While falling, the character uses their hands to catch onto another ledge before landing" — [PARC §7.1](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2505.04002)
- Running into a wall appears only as a generator failure PARC had to fix: an unconstrained generator produces "motions that do not comply with the surrounding terrain such as running into a wall instead of climbing over it", and motions that violate constraints "such as running through a wall, cannot be reproduced" by the physics tracker — [PARC §5.2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2505.04002)
- Fatigue (cumulative, in RL controllers): "Discovering Fatigued Movements for Virtual Character Animation" (Cheema, Xu, Kim, Hämäläinen, Golyanik, Habermann, Theobalt, Slusallek; SIGGRAPH Asia 2023) uses GAIL for an expert policy and a Three-Compartment Controller (3CC) model that turns "constant torque bounds based on endurance time to non-linear, state- and time-dependent limits in the joint-actuation space", letting agents "adapt to different fatigue and rest rates interactively, and discover realistic recovery strategies" — [arXiv 2310.08583](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.08583); [DOI 10.1145/3610548.3618176](https://doi.org/10.1145/3610548.3618176)
- Fatigue (2026): "Tired Actor: Fatigue-Informed Character Control" (Zhang et al., arXiv Aug 2026) again uses the 3CC model "to develop a policy enabling general motion imitation across varying fatigue levels" and shows fatigue "could influence the naturalness, scalability, and robustness of character animation" — [arXiv 2608.03528](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.03528)
- Terrain robustness caveats: CALM anticipates failure on "climbing stairs or walking on uneven terrain" — [CALM §Limitations](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2305.02195); MaskedMimic's character on rough terrain "tends to mimic standard walking motions rather than planning more suitable foot placements" — [MaskedMimic §Limitations](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2409.14393); DReCon walks over slopes and obstacles only "as a consequence of the robustness of our controller" — [DReCon §8](https://static-wordpress.ubisoft.com/montreal.ubisoft.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/11/13214229/DReCon.pdf)

### Inferences
- The game's gating rule ("high jumps before fall tolerance; sprint-into-wall kills until impact tolerance") maps cleanly onto two published knobs: joint torque limits/strength (Learning to Get Up's strong-to-weak curriculum) and time-varying actuation limits (3CC fatigue). A per-joint "strength budget" that grows with powers, plus a fatigue/impact state that temporarily lowers it, is reproducible from those citations even with a hand-authored controller.
- Hard-landing behavior is not measured in any paper as a landing-height or impact-velocity envelope; the closest quantitative proxies are the push-force tables (DeepMimic) and projectile tests (DReCon/SuperTrack). A design document should therefore define its own landing-impact metric (e.g., pelvis vertical velocity at contact and peak contact impulse) and treat published push tolerances as order-of-magnitude anchors only.
- Ledge catching in PARC is a long-horizon, terrain-conditioned skill that took a month of GPU time to emerge; for a first-person game where hands are the main visible body part, an explicit hand-contact grab (kinematic attach or constraint) is far cheaper and remains consistent with the physics-based framing.

### Gaps
- No paper was found that models injury (damage, breaking bones, health loss) as a function of impact; "cast"/missing-limb variants in Learning to Get Up are the only documented constraint-adaptation results. Search budget expired before a dedicated injury/biomechanics-impact search could be run.
- No learned controller was found that treats wall collision as an intentional event (brace, self-arrest, vault); PARC treats it as an error to remove.

## Key question 4 — Runtime path into a game engine: model sizes, inference frequency, ONNX Runtime vs custom C++ (GDExtension), CPU cost per frame, shipped games

### Takeaway
Policies used for physics control are tiny MLPs (DReCon 2×128; DeepMimic/AMP/PHC 1024–512; SuperTrack 5×1024 ≈ 20 MB) evaluated at 20–30 Hz, so inference is not the bottleneck — measured costs are 60 µs (DReCon) to ~1.8 ms (SuperTrack) per evaluation on a single Xeon core, versus 200–1000 µs per physics step. Godot has no first-party inference path: Godot RL Agents' ONNX inference requires the .NET build, and the native ONNX Runtime GDExtension in the community is described as unmaintained since Feb 2024, so a small custom C++ MLP or ncnn/ORT wrapped in a GDExtension is the practical route. One shipped title with learned physics-based locomotion was verified: ARC Raiders (Embark Studios, UE5, 30 Oct 2025), whose ML research lead says DRL is "strictly for locomotion" of the ARC enemy machines, built by a 5–10 person team "for years" and "almost cut multiple times".

### Cited Findings
- Inference cost anchors: DReCon "Policy Evaluation 60µs" out of a 340 µs total per frame for a 2×128 tanh MLP on an Intel Xeon E5-1650 v3, single-threaded — [DReCon Table 4](https://static-wordpress.ubisoft.com/montreal.ubisoft.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/11/13214229/DReCon.pdf); SuperTrack "Policy network evaluation … takes ∼1800µs, and requires ∼20MB of storage" for 5×1024 ELU on a Xeon E5-1650 — [SuperTrack §5.3](https://theorangeduck.com/media/uploads/other_stuff/SuperTrack.pdf); LMM's networks run single-threaded through "a custom, optimized neural network inference library" and its authors found weights "could be effectively compressed as 16-bit integers without significant loss" — [LMM §6.2](https://theorangeduck.com/media/uploads/other_stuff/Learned_Motion_Matching.pdf)
- Control rates in the surveyed controllers: 30 Hz policy (DeepMimic, AMP, ASE, CALM, PHC, PULSE, MaskedMimic, PhysHOI, PARC, Motivo), 20 Hz (ControlVAE), 40 Hz (Learning to Get Up), 6 Hz high-level latent (ASE, CALM); physics 60 Hz (DReCon, PHC, PULSE, PhysHOI), 120 Hz (ASE, MaskedMimic, PARC, ControlVAE), 240 Hz (SuperTrack), 450 Hz (Motivo), 800 Hz (Get Up), 1.2 kHz (DeepMimic, AMP) — sources as cited in Key question 2
- PHC "runs at > 30 FPS" once trained on the GPU-simulated avatar setup — [PHC](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2305.06456); ControlVAE "runs faster than real-time on a desktop" — [ControlVAE](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2210.06063); Motivo's zero-shot reward inference and rollout of one 300-step episode takes "12 seconds … (about 7, 3 and 2 seconds for reward relabeling, inference, and policy rollout)" — [Motivo](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2504.11054)
- Godot RL Agents (Beeching, Dibangoye, Simonin, Wolf, arXiv 2021): wraps Stable-Baselines3, Sample Factory, Ray RLlib and CleanRL; offers "experimental support for onnx models" loaded "via the Godot editor's mono version"; "With the default SB3 training script, it's normal for the Godot env to periodically freeze while the model is updating" — [godot_rl_agents README](https://github.com/edbeeching/godot_rl_agents); [arXiv 2112.03636](https://arxiv.org/abs/2112.03636)
- Community runtime comparison for Godot (godot-native-rl docs): "all three are fast for the small MLPs that RL policies usually are"; RL policy files 21.3 KB (ONNX) vs 10.4 KB (ncnn); binaries ncnn ~3.4 MB vs ONNX Runtime "~3.3 MB (minimal mobile build) to ~12 MB (full)"; stock ONNX in godot_rl "Requires Godot Mono/.NET (blocks web export)"; the native ORT GDExtension is "Community option, but 'last commit Feb 2024, no releases'"; console targets favor ncnn because "No managed runtime" — [ncnn_vs_onnx.md](https://github.com/minigraphx/godot-native-rl/blob/main/docs/ncnn_vs_onnx.md)
- Other Godot inference extensions found: godot_onnx_extension ("model.run(PackedFloat32Array)") — [repo](https://github.com/joemarshall/godot_onnx_extension/); Godot-ONNX-AI-Models-Loaders (ONNXLoader node with load_model/predict) — [repo](https://github.com/mat490/Godot-ONNX-AI-Models-Loaders); godot-infer ("GPU-accelerated neural network inference for Godot, built as a GDExtension") — [repo](https://github.com/FrancoYudica/godot-infer)
- Shipped game evidence: ARC Raiders (Embark Studios) released 30 October 2025 on PS5, Windows, Xbox Series X/S on Unreal Engine 5; "14 million copies sold worldwide by February 2026" — [Wikipedia: ARC Raiders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ARC_Raiders); Embark is a Nexon subsidiary founded 2018 that emphasizes "emerging tools such as machine learning" — [Wikipedia: Embark Studios](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Embark_Studios)
- In an 80.lv interview (31 March 2026, David Jagneaux), Embark ML Research Lead Martin Singh-Blom says of the ARC enemies: "The machine learning part is actually more limited than people think. It's strictly for locomotion, such as how the robot places its feet and moves"; behavior trees decide intent while the ML locomotion executes; enemies are physical entities so shooting them means they "might get bumped out of [their] path"; quality was improved using "adversarial motion priors"; training used controlled scenarios with randomized object placement, not online learning; a 5–10 person team worked "for years" and the system "was almost cut multiple times" — [80.lv article](https://80.lv/articles/inside-the-magic-of-machine-learning-that-powers-enemy-ai-in-arc-raiders)
- Ubisoft's DReCon page states that For Honor used Motion Matching but does not claim DReCon shipped; the SuperTrack page mentions no production integration — [DReCon page](https://www.ubisoft.com/en-us/studio/laforge/news/VjEIwquaIyEZZSw5RZI0V/drecon-datadriven-responsive-control-of-physicsbased-characters); [SuperTrack page](https://www.ubisoft.com/en-us/studio/laforge/news/7fMzaMaDgnd0gqPsCaJZYb/supertrack-motion-tracking-for-physically-simulated-characters-using-supervised-learning)
- EA SEED's publications page lists physics-based RL character research co-authored with academia — "Learning soccer juggling skills with layer-wise mixture-of-experts" (Xie, Starke, Ling, van de Panne, SIGGRAPH 2022) — alongside kinematic ML animation (DeepPhase, Neural animation layering, Local motion phases) and "Neural network cloth simulation in Madden NFL 21", but no shipped physics-based learned character controller — [EA SEED publications](https://www.ea.com/seed/publications)
- The 2022 STAR's assessment: "Deep Reinforcement Learning is still a relatively young field, and thus its use is not common in the industry. This is likely to change in the upcoming years" — [Kwiatkowski et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2203.04735)
- Jolt Physics (the engine in the user's fork) ships in Horizon Forbidden West and Death Stranding 2, is MIT-licensed C++17, and provides swing-twist ("for humanoid shoulders"), hinge, 6-DOF and fixed constraints, constraint motors with position/velocity and spring settings, a Ragdoll class with RagdollSettings, a skeleton mapper for "Mapping a high detail (animation) skeleton onto a low detail (ragdoll) skeleton", hard/soft keying and motor-driven pose following, and deterministic simulation — [JoltPhysics README](https://github.com/jrouwe/JoltPhysics)
- Godot's Jolt integration became built-in in Godot 4.4 (Project Settings > Physics > 3D > Physics Engine), but the docs list unsupported joint features: Generic6DOFJoint3D lacks softness, restitution, damping and ERP limit settings; HingeJoint3D and ConeTwistJoint3D have no motor or softness parameters ("a warning is emitted if you set these properties to anything but their default values"); multithreading is "experimental" and "has not been tested very thoroughly" — [Godot docs: Using Jolt Physics](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/latest/tutorials/physics/using_jolt_physics.html)

### Inferences
- For a 25–30 DOF ragdoll, a hand-written C++ forward pass of a 2×128 or 2×256 MLP (a few hundred thousand multiply-adds) inside a GDExtension will cost tens of microseconds per 30 Hz tick, on the order of DReCon's 60 µs; adopting ONNX Runtime buys convenience for re-training iteration but adds a 3–12 MB dependency and a .NET requirement in the stock Godot RL Agents path. Since the user's build is a custom Godot fork with Jolt, exposing Jolt's own constraint motors, RagdollSettings and SkeletonMapper to GDScript/C++ (which upstream Godot does not expose) is likely the highest-leverage engine work, independent of whether learning is used.
- ARC Raiders is the only verified shipped title with RL-trained physics locomotion, and its lessons are sobering for a small team: multi-year effort by a dedicated 5–10 person ML team, scope restricted to enemy locomotion (not the player), decision-making left to behavior trees, and near-cancellation. A single-player first-person player character is a harder target because the camera makes every artifact visible and latency is felt directly.

### Gaps
- No primary Embark source (talk, paper, blog) was reachable: Embark's Medium blog and the Unreal Engine developer interview returned HTTP 403, and search budget expired; the 80.lv interview is the only source and is secondary (though quoting Embark's ML lead directly). The training simulator, network sizes and runtime costs for ARC Raiders are unknown.
- No evidence was found for a shipped Sony or EA title using a learned physics-based character controller; the Sony search could not be run (budget). Absence of evidence here is not evidence of absence.
- No benchmark of ONNX Runtime CPU latency specifically inside Godot was found; the only measured per-frame costs are the Ubisoft papers' custom inference libraries.

## Key question 5 — Training tooling (Isaac Gym/Lab, MuJoCo, PyBullet, Brax), training against Jolt/Godot, and sim-to-sim gap

### Takeaway
Every 2022+ controller surveyed was trained in a GPU-parallel simulator (Isaac Gym/Flex/Isaac Lab, 2k–16k environments) or MuJoCo; the 2019–2021 game-oriented work (DReCon, SuperTrack) trained directly in Bullet/PhysX/Havok on CPUs at 2,500–5,000 samples/s. Training against Godot+Jolt is possible in principle through Godot RL Agents' socket interface (~12k interactions/s across 4 processes on a laptop) but is two to three orders of magnitude slower than Isaac Gym. Training elsewhere and deploying into Jolt runs into a documented sim-to-sim gap: a September 2026 Isaac Lab issue shows a PhysX-trained tracker transferring to MuJoCo while a hierarchical AMP policy on top "collapsed within 0.9–2 seconds", and PolySim (2025) reports a 52.8% success improvement in MuJoCo only after training across several simulators.

### Cited Findings
- Isaac Gym scale: ASE trains "4096 environments … on a single NVIDIA V100 GPU" — [ASE](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2205.01906); CALM 4096 environments on one A100 for 5 billion steps — [CALM](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2305.02195); PhysHOI 2048 environments on one A100 — [PhysHOI](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2312.04393); MaskedMimic "16,384 parallel environments, split across 4 A100 GPUs … approximately 2 weeks" — [MaskedMimic](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2409.14393); PARC one A6000 for ~one month over three augmentation iterations — [PARC](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2505.04002)
- CPU-era costs: DeepMimic ~2 days on 8 CPU cores per skill (Bullet) — [DeepMimic](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1804.02717); AMP 30–140 h on 16 CPU cores (Bullet) — [AMP](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2104.02180); DReCon 8 parallel Bullet sims at 2,500 steps/s, ~30 h — [DReCon](https://static-wordpress.ubisoft.com/montreal.ubisoft.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/11/13214229/DReCon.pdf); SuperTrack ~5,000 samples/s, 20–40 h, first balance in ~2 h on a GTX 1070 — [SuperTrack](https://theorangeduck.com/media/uploads/other_stuff/SuperTrack.pdf); ControlVAE ~50 h with 4 CPU threads + one 2080Ti (ODE) — [ControlVAE](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2210.06063); Learning to Get Up ~24 h per policy on a 32-core CPU + RTX 2070 (MuJoCo) — [Get Up](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2205.00307)
- Game-engine physics as the training simulator: SuperTrack was trained and evaluated in PhysX, Havok and Bullet with "reasonably similar" final performance, noting that "Bullet and Havok do not easily support PD target rotational velocities" — [SuperTrack §5.1.3](https://theorangeduck.com/media/uploads/other_stuff/SuperTrack.pdf); the same paper motivates this with "Physics engines developed for games [Coumans 2015; Havok 2021; PhysX 2021] are a good example of this" (fast sample generation) — [SuperTrack §2](https://theorangeduck.com/media/uploads/other_stuff/SuperTrack.pdf)
- Godot as an RL environment: Godot RL Agents connects the engine to Python "with a TCP socket client-server relationship"; exported environments enable "accelerated, faster than real-time physics and headless training"; benchmark "12k environment interactions per second with up to 4 parallel processes" on a laptop, with an action repeat of 4 — [Godot RL Agents paper](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2112.03636)
- Engine review (2024): of nine engines (Brax, Chrono, Gazebo, MuJoCo, ODE, PhysX, PyBullet, Webots, Unity), "MuJoCo [is] the leading framework due to its performance and flexibility, despite usability challenges. Unity is noted for its ease of use but lacks scalability and simulation fidelity"; Nvidia Isaac usage in ML papers rose "from 20 to 286" over the surveyed period; Godot and Jolt are not covered — [A Review of Nine Physics Engines for RL Research](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2407.08590)
- Sim-to-sim failure report (Isaac Lab issue #7750, 10 Sept 2026): a BeyondMimic/ProtoMotions G1 tracker trained in PhysX "successfully transferred … to MuJoCo" (stable, z ≈ 0.78) but the hierarchical system with a "PhysX-trained AMP 'run' actor layered atop the tracker … collapsed within 0.9–2 seconds in MuJoCo across all tested conditions"; setup was implicit PD at PhysX 2000 Hz with 4× decimation → 50 Hz control; the author "systematically eliminated contact models, solver parameters, friction settings, action EMA smoothing, and target-frame parameterizations without resolving the instability" — [IsaacLab issue 7750](https://github.com/isaac-sim/IsaacLab/issues/7750)
- PolySim (2025) argues single-simulator training is "structurally limited" because "the transition kernel" of one engine is baked in; training across IsaacGym, IsaacSim and Genesis "increases the motion-tracking execution success rate by 52.8% over an IsaacSim-only baseline in MuJoCo" — [PolySim](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.01708)
- Engine-specific hacks leak into policies: PhysHOI needed force sensors because "Isaac Gym does not yet provide contact detection APIs for the GPU pipeline" — [PhysHOI](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2312.04393); Motivo builds on a custom SMPL MuJoCo model with hand-designed "joint ranges and PD controllers" — [Motivo App. C](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2504.11054); DReCon lowered Bullet's ERP to soften constraints at 60 Hz — [DReCon §7.6](https://static-wordpress.ubisoft.com/montreal.ubisoft.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/11/13214229/DReCon.pdf)
- Jolt provides deterministic simulation and constraint motors with spring settings — [JoltPhysics README](https://github.com/jrouwe/JoltPhysics) — but Godot's binding does not expose motors on HingeJoint3D/ConeTwistJoint3D or 6-DOF softness/damping/ERP — [Godot docs](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/latest/tutorials/physics/using_jolt_physics.html)

### Inferences
- Training directly in the target engine is the only way to avoid the sim-to-sim gap entirely, and Ubisoft's two papers prove it is workable with a game-class solver at 60–240 Hz if the team writes a headless C++ harness. For Godot+Jolt that means bypassing the Godot scene tree (Godot RL Agents' ~12k interactions/s across processes vs. millions per second on a GPU simulator) and driving Jolt directly from a standalone C++ trainer, or training in MuJoCo/Isaac and accepting a transfer step with domain randomization of gains, friction, mass and contact parameters.
- The hierarchical-policy transfer failure in issue #7750 is directly relevant to the ASE/CALM/PULSE design pattern (latent high-level policy over a low-level tracker): the low-level tracker is the transferable part; anything trained on top of its engine-specific dynamics is fragile.

### Gaps
- No published work trains a physics character controller in Godot or Jolt; Godot RL Agents' physics-character examples are simple (e.g., "Jumper", "Ball Chase"), and its ONNX path is described as experimental.
- Brax was mentioned only in the engine review; no character-animation controller in this survey used it.

## Key question 6 — Verdict for a small team: learned controller now, or hand-authored PD/feedback base with learning later?

### Takeaway
Hand-authored PD/feedback control should be the base. The evidence is that even the cheapest learned controllers cost 30–140 CPU-hours per skill plus weeks of reward/curriculum engineering, they are trained outside the shipping engine and transfer imperfectly, their own authors report jitter/foot-sliding/failure on dynamic skills, and the one shipped example (ARC Raiders) took a dedicated 5–10 person ML team years for enemy locomotion only. The surveyed papers also show what to hand-author: PD targets from a kinematic pose at 30 Hz, stable-PD or velocity-motor joints at 60–240 Hz, torque limits as the "strength" knob, projectile/push perturbations as the robustness test, and a state machine for fall/get-up — every one of which is also the interface a learned policy would plug into later.

### Cited Findings
- Cheapest learned-controller budgets: DeepMimic "about 2 days on an 8-core machine" per skill — [DeepMimic](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1804.02717); AMP "30-140 hours on 16 CPU cores" per policy — [AMP](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2104.02180); DReCon "around 30 hours" for locomotion only — [DReCon](https://static-wordpress.ubisoft.com/montreal.ubisoft.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/11/13214229/DReCon.pdf); SuperTrack "∼20 to ∼40 hours" and its authors warn training is "an already long training time" sensitive to learning rate and window sizes — [SuperTrack](https://theorangeduck.com/media/uploads/other_stuff/SuperTrack.pdf)
- Production risk in the authors' words: SuperTrack notes RL's "unpredictability of the algorithm's success can make it a risky choice in a production setting" — [SuperTrack §1](https://theorangeduck.com/media/uploads/other_stuff/SuperTrack.pdf); the 2022 STAR lists the many design decisions "beyond the choice of algorithm" (torque limits, friction, mass distribution, joint damping, time steps, action space, state representation, reward balancing) — [Kwiatkowski et al. 2022 §6.2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2203.04735)
- Quality residuals reported by the strongest models: ASE "jittering, sudden jerks, and overly energetic recovery behaviors" — [ASE](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2205.01906); MaskedMimic "unnatural jittering", backflips/breakdance failures, naive terrain footwork — [MaskedMimic](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2409.14393); Motivo "unnatural movements, particularly for behaviors like falling or standing" — [Motivo](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2504.11054); PHC 98.9% (not 100%) and struggles with backflips — [PHC](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2305.06456)
- Shipped-game reality check: ML at Embark is "strictly for locomotion", not decisions; 5–10 people "for years"; "almost cut multiple times" — [80.lv](https://80.lv/articles/inside-the-magic-of-machine-learning-that-powers-enemy-ai-in-arc-raiders)
- The reusable non-learned scaffolding the papers rely on: stable PD control (Tan et al. 2011) at large time steps — [DeepMimic](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1804.02717), [ControlVAE](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2210.06063); velocity-constraint motors clamped to 200 Nm at 60 Hz — [DReCon](https://static-wordpress.ubisoft.com/montreal.ubisoft.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/11/13214229/DReCon.pdf); "Well-designed torque limits play an essential role in the quality of motion. Poorly designed torque limits can lead to unnatural motion" — [Get Up §4](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2205.00307); the game-controller-style FSM/behavior-tree interface over a low-level controller — [CALM §Limitations](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2305.02195)
- Jolt already ships the ragdoll primitives a hand-authored controller needs (constraint motors, Ragdoll/RagdollSettings, skeleton mapper, hard/soft keying) and is used in Horizon Forbidden West and Death Stranding 2 — [JoltPhysics README](https://github.com/jrouwe/JoltPhysics)

### Inferences
- A defensible roadmap reproducible from these citations: (1) ragdoll with Jolt swing-twist/hinge constraints and motors, driven by PD targets from a kinematic pose at 30 Hz with physics at 120–240 Hz (SuperTrack/DReCon rates); (2) per-joint torque limits as the power-progression variable (Learning to Get Up), with a 3CC-style fatigue/impact multiplier (Cheema 2023) implementing consequence gating; (3) DeepMimic-style push tests (N × 0.2 s at the pelvis) and DReCon/SuperTrack projectile tests as the acceptance metrics for "realistic reactions"; (4) explicit fall detection (torso/head contact, as DeepMimic's early-termination criterion) triggering a get-up state; (5) only after this works, consider a learned residual policy (DReCon's "corrective changes to the PD control targets") trained in a headless Jolt harness, because that is the smallest network (2×128) with the best documented runtime cost and the lowest transfer risk.
- Because the game is first-person and mostly hands/legs/camera are visible, the visual payoff of full-body learned motion quality is lower than in third-person, while the cost of latency and jitter (camera shake, hand IK) is higher — this further favors deterministic hand-authored feedback for the player body, with learned controllers (if any) reserved for NPC/enemy bodies, mirroring ARC Raiders' scoping.

### Gaps
- No source quantifies first-person-specific perceptual thresholds for physics-driven camera/hand motion; the Tollola review's 6.04 ms touch-feedback figure concerns projection displays, not embodied cameras, and should not be reused for this purpose.
