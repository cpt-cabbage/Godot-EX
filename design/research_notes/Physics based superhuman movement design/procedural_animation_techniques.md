## Key Question 2: Inverse kinematics — FABRIK, CCD, two-bone analytic, Jacobian/DLS, foot and hand IK, costs, and what Godot ships

### Takeaway
Use analytic two-bone IK (law of cosines) for legs and arms because it is closed-form and deterministic; use FABRIK or CCD only for longer chains (spine, tails, multi-segment reach), with 4–10 iterations and a ~1 mm–1 cm tolerance; Jacobian methods buy full-body coupling at much higher cost. Godot 4.6+ ships all of these as `IKModifier3D` subclasses (TwoBoneIK3D, FABRIK3D, CCDIK3D, JacobianIK3D, ChainIK3D, SplineIK3D), so a Godot 4.8-dev fork needs no third-party solver.

### Cited Findings
- FABRIK (Forward And Backward Reaching IK): A. Aristidou and J. Lasenby, "FABRIK: A fast, iterative solver for the Inverse Kinematics problem", *Graphical Models* 73(5):243–260, 2011, DOI 10.1016/j.gmod.2011.05.003 (454 citations per Semantic Scholar). Abstract claims: "avoids the use of rotational angles or matrices, and instead finds each joint position via locating a point on a line", "converges in few iterations, has low computational cost and produces visually realistic poses", constraints "can easily be incorporated" and "multiple chains with multiple end effectors are also supported". — [ACM DL abstract page](https://dl.acm.org/doi/abs/10.1016/j.gmod.2011.05.003); [author page](https://andreasaristidou.com/FABRIK) (503 this session); [Semantic Scholar API record](https://api.semanticscholar.org/graph/v1/paper/DOI:10.1016/j.gmod.2011.05.003)
- FABRIK algorithm (standard formulation, not verified against the PDF this session because the PDF was unreachable): forward stage sets the end effector to the target and walks toward the root, placing each joint on the line between the newly placed child and its old position at the original bone length; backward stage resets the root and walks outward the same way; repeat until the tip is within tolerance; if the target is farther than the summed bone lengths the chain is simply straightened toward it. — [Aristidou & Lasenby 2011](https://dl.acm.org/doi/abs/10.1016/j.gmod.2011.05.003)
- Godot's own FABRIK3D documentation records a known FABRIK artefact: "When the target is close to the root, it tends to produce zig-zag patterns, resulting in unnatural visual movement", and notes it "works best with simple chains lacking constraints". — [Godot FABRIK3D class reference](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/latest/classes/class_fabrik3d.html)
- CCD (Cyclic Coordinate Descent): originally Wang & Chen 1991; it "adjust[s] one degree of freedom at a time to move the end effector toward the target... one equation in one unknown for each degree of freedom", a Jacobi/Gauss-Seidel-type relaxation. — [Canutescu & Dunbrack 2003, PMC (describing Wang & Chen 1991)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2323867/). Game-oriented treatment: Ben Kenwright, "Inverse Kinematics – Cyclic Coordinate Descent (CCD)", *Journal of Graphics Tools* 16(4):177–217, 2012, DOI 10.1080/2165347X.2013.823362 (abstract page returned 403; page range from the arXiv paper's reference list). — [Taylor & Francis DOI](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/2165347X.2013.823362); [arXiv 2302.10769 reference list](https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.10769)
- Godot CCDIK3D notes CCD gives "smoother and more stable target tracking compared to FABRIK3D" on chains with rotational constraints but "joint flips and oscillations" when the target is near the root. — [Godot CCDIK3D class reference](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/latest/classes/class_ccdik3d.html)
- Two-bone analytic IK (2D form, Ryan Juckett, 29 Dec 2008, updated 2020): cos θ₂ = (x² + y² − d₁² − d₂²)/(2 d₁ d₂); θ₁ = atan2(ẏ, ẋ) with ẋ = x(d₁ + d₂cos θ₂) + y(d₂ sin θ₂), ẏ = y(d₁ + d₂cos θ₂) − x(d₂ sin θ₂); reachable range |d₁ − d₂| ≤ dist ≤ d₁ + d₂; clamp cos θ₂ to [−1,1] for unreachable targets; negate θ₂ for the opposite bend. — [Juckett, Analytic Two-Bone IK in 2D](https://www.ryanjuckett.com/analytic-two-bone-ik-in-2d/)
- Two-joint IK in 3D (Daniel Holden): clamp target distance to [eps, lab + lcb − eps] with eps = 0.01; current angles via acos of dot products; desired angles by law of cosines, hip: acos(clamp((lcb² − lab² − lat²)/(−2 lab lat), −1, 1)), knee: acos(clamp((lat² − lab² − lcb²)/(−2 lab lcb), −1, 1)); apply Δangle about the bend axis in local space (`r0 = quat_angle_axis(desired − current, quat_mul(quat_inv(a_gr), axis0))`), then rotate the hip to align the chain with the target; stabilise the bend axis with a pole vector d: `axis0 = normalize(cross(c − a, d))`, typically the knee's forward direction. — [Holden, Simple Two Joint IK](https://theorangeduck.com/page/simple-two-joint)
- Godot TwoBoneIK3D solves by "intersection of two circles", takes root/middle/end joints plus a pole target that must not be colinear with the forward vector, and treats intermediate bones as virtual straight segments. — [Godot TwoBoneIK3D class reference](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/latest/classes/class_twoboneik3d.html)
- Jacobian family (standard formulation from S. R. Buss, "Introduction to inverse kinematics with Jacobian transpose, pseudoinverse and damped least squares methods", UCSD manuscript, April 2004 — PDF returned 503 this session, so formulas are not verified against it): Jacobian transpose Δθ = α Jᵀ e; pseudoinverse Δθ = J⁺ e; damped least squares Δθ = Jᵀ(J Jᵀ + λ² I)⁻¹ e, with λ trading accuracy for stability near singularities; Buss & Kim's Selectively Damped Least Squares (J. Graphics Tools 10(3):37–49, 2005) damps per singular value. — [Buss IK methods page](https://mathweb.ucsd.edu/~sbuss/ResearchWeb/ikmethods/index.html); [SDLS paper PDF](https://mathweb.ucsd.edu/~sbuss/ResearchWeb/ikmethods/SdlsPaper.pdf)
- Godot JacobianIK3D is "Jacobian transpose based", computes all joints simultaneously, and "converges gradually, resulting in gentler and less immediate tracking". — [Godot JacobianIK3D class reference](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/latest/classes/class_jacobianik3d.html)
- Measured comparison on a 3-DOF planar human leg (MATLAB; 0.5 s trajectory at 1.33 m/s): computation time CCD 0.0061 s (RMSE 0.0982), Moore-Penrose pseudoinverse 2.78 s (0.0603), Levenberg-Marquardt DLS 2.05 s (0.0299), constrained optimisation 0.036 s (9.1e-6), genetic MOOGA 0.59 s (1.3e-4), neural network 0.0083 s (9.7e-7); CCD "causes the links to rotate in a sequence that does not match natural movement" and its comfort index diverged because joints sat at their limits. — [Benhmidouch, Moufid & Ait Omar, arXiv 2302.10769v4, Table 4](https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.10769)
- Measured comparison in a game-style C++ loop (Deglorie 2015): Jacobian Transpose 1250 ticks/frame, CCD 300 ticks/frame, mean error 0.53 cm vs 0.72 cm. — [Deglorie et al. 2015, Table 3](https://www.scitepress.org/papers/2015/53106/53106.pdf)
- Spore's production solver (Hecker et al. 2008) rejected CCD/Jacobian/constrained dynamics as "slower and less amenable to tuning" and used a particle-and-length-constraint solver (Jakobsen-style nonlinear length correction) in two phases (spine, then limbs): 5 outer relaxation iterations, simplified limbs allowed to compress to 10% of rest length in the spine phase, final projection to a 10%/120% shrink/stretch window, an "aim preconditioner" that rotates/scales the whole limb onto its goal before iterating; whole character (25 bodies) cost ≈0.2 ms/frame on a 1.7 GHz Pentium-M with ≈35% in IK. — [Hecker et al. 2008, §4.3, §5](https://www.chrishecker.com/images/c/cb/Sporeanim-siggraph08.pdf)
- Godot 4.6 added `IKModifier3D` with subclasses TwoBoneIK3D, ChainIK3D, IterateIK3D, CCDIK3D, FABRIK3D, JacobianIK3D, SplineIK3D (PR #110120 by TokageItLab). IterateIK3D defaults: `max_iterations` 4, `min_distance` 0.001, `angular_delta_limit` 0.034906585 rad (≈2°), `deterministic` false; when joint limits are applied "the IK tip may not reach the target". — [Godot 4.6 dev 4 snapshot](https://godotengine.org/article/dev-snapshot-godot-4-6-dev-4/); [PR #110120](https://github.com/godotengine/godot/pull/110120); [Godot IterateIK3D class reference](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/latest/classes/class_iterateik3d.html); [Godot IKModifier3D class reference](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/latest/classes/class_ikmodifier3d.html)
- Legacy `SkeletonIK3D` (still present, "may be changed or removed") uses FABRIK with defaults `max_iterations` 10, `min_distance` 0.01, optional `magnet` pole target, `override_tip_basis` true. — [Godot SkeletonIK3D class reference](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/stable/classes/class_skeletonik3d.html)
- Foot placement on slopes/stairs (procedure): raycast "downward from the character's knees or hips toward the ground" to get hit point and normal; "the pelvis must be lowered... so the legs have enough slack to reach their respective ground targets"; rotate the foot bone to the surface normal so the sole lies flat; lerp from the animated pose into the IK pose over several frames; solve with two-bone IK or FABRIK. No numeric parameters are given in that source. — [Foot-Placement IK for Uneven Terrain (salivity.github.io)](https://salivity.github.io/game-development/article/foot-placement-inverse-kinematics-for-uneven-terrain)
- Pelvis rule stated numerically in a secondary source: the pelvis moves down by the lowest foot's offset ("if the left foot needs to go 15cm lower on the downhill side, the entire body drops 15cm, and the right foot then gets a positive IK offset to compensate"). — [StraySpark, Procedural Animation and IK in UE5.7 (search snippet; page not opened)](https://www.strayspark.studio/blog/procedural-animation-ik-ue57-guide)
- Foot locking with continuity: Holden's controller switches the foot IK input between the animated foot and "a state where we feed as input just that fixed contact point (with zero velocity)" and inertializes the switch (code default `ik_blending_halflife = 0.1` s). — [Holden, Code vs Data Driven Displacement](https://theorangeduck.com/page/code-vs-data-driven-displacement); [controller.cpp](https://github.com/orangeduck/Motion-Matching)
- Ubisoft's IK Rig (Bereznyak, GDC 2016) represents a pose as IK chains with active bones (hands, feet) as effectors plus head, chest and hips, and its "Nostradamus mode" bakes footstep data with the animation, traces new desired foot placement positions and reconstructs footstep curves for terrain; solver type and iteration counts are not stated in the slides. — [GDC Vault](https://www.gdcvault.com/play/1023279/IK-Rig-Procedural-Pose); [slide text on archive.org](https://archive.org/stream/GDC2016Bereznyak/GDC2016-Bereznyak_djvu.txt)
- Aligning hands/feet to ledges and vault edges in production: Gears of War 4 places authored "warp points" in animation scenes matching physical landmarks; at runtime the game places runtime warp points in the world and warps the character's trajectory so authored and runtime points coincide, with independent timing control for translation, rotation and facing; this replaced blend spaces that cost 20 µs per sequence + 10 µs per blend on Xbox One (1D blend space = 150% overhead, 2D = 300%). — [Bollo, SIGGRAPH 2017 Talks, DOI 10.1145/3084363.3085069](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3084363.3085069); [PDF](https://history.siggraph.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/09/2017-Talks-Bollo_High-Performance-Animation-in-Gears-of-War-4.pdf)
- Secondary source on limb solver choice for first-person: "foot IK only needs a two-bone solver (thigh-shin-foot), and hand IK for simple reach only needs a two-bone solver". — [StraySpark UE5.7 guide (search snippet)](https://www.strayspark.studio/blog/procedural-animation-ik-ue57-guide)

### Inferences
- Iteration budgets: Godot's shipped defaults (4 iterations, 1 mm tolerance for iterative solvers; 10 iterations, 1 cm for legacy FABRIK) and Spore's 5 outer relaxation passes bracket the practical range for a humanoid; two-bone limbs need zero iterations.
- The cost ordering is consistent across three independent measurements (Deglorie 2015, Benhmidouch 2024, Hecker 2008): analytic/particle ≪ CCD ≪ Jacobian-family. For a superhuman first-person character where every frame also runs Jolt, prefer analytic two-bone for legs/arms, particle/FABRIK chains for spine-lean and a Jacobian-transpose solver only for optional whole-body reach.
- The "pelvis drops to the lowest foot" rule is what makes stairs and slopes read as weighty; combine it with Bollo-style clamped inertialization on the pelvis offset so a superhumanly fast character does not pop when foot targets jump.

## Key Question 3: Procedural locomotion — Overgrowth, Rain World, Spore, tutorials, gait timing vs speed, and scaling a gait beyond human speed

### Takeaway
Two production-proven procedural gait designs exist: sparse-keyframe interpolation driven by a physics "vehicle" (Rosen/Overgrowth, 13 keyframes total) and parametric gait generators driven by speed (Spore's duty-factor/step-trigger tables; Fu's CPG). Human biomechanics gives the speed→gait mapping: cadence rises only slowly with speed (≈169→178 steps/min from 2.7→3.8 m/s), the walk–run switch happens near Froude 0.5 (≈2.0–2.1 m/s for a 0.9 m leg), and top sprint speed is limited by ground force, not leg-swing rate — which tells you exactly which parameters to scale when the character becomes faster than human.

### Cited Findings

**Spore (Hecker et al., SIGGRAPH 2008)**
- Chris Hecker, Bernd Raabe, Ryan W. Enslow, John DeWeese, Jordan Maynard, Kees van Prooijen, "Real-time Motion Retargeting to Highly Varied User-Created Morphologies", *ACM Transactions on Graphics* 27(3) (SIGGRAPH 2008). — [Author page with PDF and videos](https://www.chrishecker.com/Real-time_Motion_Retargeting_to_Highly_Varied_User-Created_Morphologies); [PDF](https://www.chrishecker.com/images/c/cb/Sporeanim-siggraph08.pdf)
- Gait system: legs are paths from a foot body to a spine "hip"; legs are clustered into groups of roughly equal length whose length ratios are approximated by small rationals to set relative gait-cycle frequencies; for each group the system assigns "the locomotion duty factor and step trigger parameters. **Gait timing as a function of speed (biomechanics)**
- Running on an instrumented treadmill, 30 experienced runners: cadence 169.2 steps/min at 2.68 m/s → 170.5 at 2.98 → 174.3 at 3.35 → 177.9 at 3.83 m/s; stride length 0.98 m at 2.68 m/s → 1.30 m at 3.83 m/s; stance time 0.28 s → 0.24 s; vertical GRF peak 2.33 → 2.54 body weights; impact peak 1.51 → 1.88 BW; average loading rate 36.8 → 48.0 BW/s. — [Leacox et al. 2025, Int J Sports Phys Ther 20(7):957–963, DOI 10.26603/001c.140544](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12222555/)
- Self-selected running stride frequency 77–80 strides/min (154–160 steps/min) across 2.38–2.9 m/s, with an energetically optimal ≈83 strides/min (≈166 steps/min) that "did not significantly" change with speed. — [van Oeveren et al. 2017, PLoS ONE 12(10):e0184273](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5653196/)
- Walk-to-run transition: preferred transition velocity 7.7 ± 0.4 km/h (≈2.14 m/s); stride frequency at transition 70.6 ± 3.2 strides/min; during walking, stride frequency rose with speed (R² = 0.976); the authors argue stride frequency predicts the transition better than the Froude number. — [Hansen et al. 2017, Scientific Reports 7:2010](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-017-01972-1)
- Froude number and the inverted-pendulum limit: "The ratio of the centripetal and gravitational forces (mv²/L)/(mg) reduces to the dimensionless Froude number (v²/gL)" with L = leg length (hip height); walking is impossible above Fr = 1 in the point-mass model; "humans and other bipeds with different leg lengths all choose to switch from a walk to a run at different absolute speeds but at approximately the same Froude number (0.5)"; measured transition ≈1.98 m/s at 1 g (subjects' mean leg length 0.89 m), Fr 0.45–0.56 from 1.0 g down to 0.4 g, 0.97 m/s at 0.1 g. — [Kram, Domingo & Ferris 1997, J Exp Biol 200:821–826](https://spot.colorado.edu/~kram/walk-run.pdf)
- Dynamic similarity hypothesis: animals "move in a dynamically similar fashion whenever they travel at speeds that give them equal values of... the Froude number", predicting equal relative stride lengths, duty factors and gait transitions at equal Fr. — [Alexander & Jayes 1983, J. Zoology 201:135–152, DOI 10.1111/j.1469-7998.1983.tb04266.x](https://zslpublications.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/j.1469-7998.1983.tb04266.x); Fr = v²/(g·h) with h a characteristic length, as restated in [Raichlen, Pontzer & Shapiro 2013, Biology Open 2:1032–1036](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3798186/)
- Top sprint speed: across 33 subjects with top speeds 6.2–11.1 m/s, mass-specific ground support force rose 1.26× from slowest to fastest while swing time (limb repositioning) "showed no significant variation"; on declines vs inclines (9.96 vs 7.10 m/s) support forces were 2.30 ± 0.06 vs 1.76 ± 0.04 body weights with similar minimum swing times. — [Weyand, Sternlight, Bellizzi & Wright 2000, J Appl Physiol 89(5):1991–1999, DOI 10.1152/jappl.2000.89.5.1991](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=DOI:10.1152/jappl.2000.89.5.1991&resultType=core&format=json)
- Walking-speed gait transitions in a CPG model can be scheduled by a sigmoid on speed: φ⁰ij(v) = φlow + (φhigh − φlow)/(1 + e^(−k(v − v0))), with coupling wij(v) increasing linearly with v. — [Fu 2025, eqs. 2.13–2.14](https://lup.lub.lu.se/luur/download?func=downloadFile&recordOId=9206764&fileOId=9206770)

### Inferences
- Speed → gait parameters, human range: speed v = step length × cadence. Fit the Leacox table (cadence ≈ 2.82 → 2.97 Hz over 2.68 → 3.83 m/s) and take the rest of the speed increase as step length; below ≈2.1 m/s switch to a walk whose stride frequency falls toward ≈70 strides/min (Hansen) and whose duty factor exceeds 0.5 (Hecker's definition). This reproduces the standard "stride length grows first, cadence later" behaviour.
- Scaling beyond human speed (design rule derived from Weyand 2000 + Kram 1997): keep swing time roughly constant (≈ the human value), shorten ground-contact time and increase step length by adding flight time — i.e. superhuman speed should look like bounding with larger vertical GRF, not like a sped-up run. Because the walk–run transition sits at Fr ≈ 0.5, a character with an effective "strength multiplier" s can be treated as living in effective gravity s·g for gait purposes: transitions and dynamically similar poses occur at speeds scaled by √s (Fr = v²/(s·g·L)). Implement gait blending on Fr = v²/(g_eff·L) rather than on raw speed so the same tables keep working as powers grow.
- Consequence hooks: the Leacox table shows impact peak and loading rate rising with speed (1.51→1.88 BW, 36.8→48 BW/s over ~1 m/s); extrapolating those trends gives the "gating by consequences" signals (landing forces, loading rate) needed for the design.
- For Godot: Fu's Hopf-oscillator CPG (two oscillators for legs, two for arms, phase-locked) is a drop-in gait clock; drive its ωi from cadence(v) and feed the phase to Rosen-style two-pose interpolation (13 keyframes) or to Spore-style duty-factor/step-trigger foot schedulers plus two-bone IK.

## Key Question 4: Motion continuity and springs — inertialization, Holden's spring-damper formulas, Verlet/spring bones, motion warping

### Takeaway
Inertialization (Bollo) removes cross-fade cost and preserves momentum by decaying the source–target pose offset with a quintic whose blend time is clamped to prevent overshoot (t₁ = min(t₁, −5x₀/v₀)); Holden's half-life-parameterised critically damped springs give the same behaviour with a single intuitive parameter and closed-form exact updates; Jakobsen's Verlet-plus-constraint-projection is the standard cheap secondary-motion model and is what Godot's `SpringBoneSimulator3D` (4.4+) approximates; motion warping (Witkin & Popović 1995) and its production descendants (warp points, Unreal Motion Warping) align authored clips to runtime targets.

### Cited Findings

**Inertialization (David Bollo, The Coalition / Microsoft)**
- Venues: "High Performance Animation in Gears of War 4", ACM SIGGRAPH 2017 Talks, DOI 10.1145/3084363.3085069 (2-page paper + 1-page supplemental with the equations); "Inertialization: High-Performance Animation Transitions in Gears of War", GDC 2018 (65 slides). The user's brief said "GDC 2016"; the accessible materials are SIGGRAPH 2017 and GDC 2018 (DReCon and Learned Motion Matching cite "Bollo 2016, 2017"). — [SIGGRAPH 2017 Talk](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3084363.3085069); [Talk PDF](https://history.siggraph.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/09/2017-Talks-Bollo_High-Performance-Animation-in-Gears-of-War-4.pdf); [Supplemental PDF](https://cdn.gearsofwar.com/thecoalition/publications/SIGGRAPH%202017%20-%20High%20Performance%20Animation%20in%20Gears%20ofWar%204%20-%20Supplemental.pdf); [GDC 2018 slides PDF](https://media.gdcvault.com/gdc2018/presentations/bollo_david_inertialization_high_performance.pdf); [GDC Vault](https://www.gdcvault.com/play/1025331/Inertialization-High-Performance-Animation-Transitions)
- Idea: "we eliminate blended transitions altogether and choose instead to handle motion transitions as an animation post-process... we simply cut immediately to the target animation and let the post-process clean up the results"; record x₀ = x_source − x_target and the source velocity v₀ (finite difference of the last two source poses; the target's velocity is deliberately not evaluated); add a decaying curve x(t) to the target pose for 0 ≤ t ≤ t₁. — [Bollo 2017 supplemental, eqs. 1–2](https://cdn.gearsofwar.com/thecoalition/publications/SIGGRAPH%202017%20-%20High%20Performance%20Animation%20in%20Gears%20ofWar%204%20-%20Supplemental.pdf)
- Overshoot guards: assume x₀ > 0 (otherwise negate x₀, v₀ and the result); if v₀ > 0 (moving away from target) clamp v₀ = 0; clamp t₁ = min(t₁, −5x₀/v₀) so any inflection lies beyond t₁. — [Bollo 2017 supplemental](https://cdn.gearsofwar.com/thecoalition/publications/SIGGRAPH%202017%20-%20High%20Performance%20Animation%20in%20Gears%20ofWar%204%20-%20Supplemental.pdf); [GDC 2018 slide 35](https://media.gdcvault.com/gdc2018/presentations/bollo_david_inertialization_high_performance.pdf)
- Initial acceleration: a₀ = (−8 v₀ t₁ − 20 x₀)/t₁² (zero jerk at t₁), used only if positive (opposing v₀), else a₀ = 0. — [GDC 2018 slide 27; supplemental eq. 3](https://media.gdcvault.com/gdc2018/presentations/bollo_david_inertialization_high_performance.pdf)
- Quintic (slide form, "Inertialization on one slide"): A = −(a₀t₁² + 6v₀t₁ + 12x₀)/(2t₁⁵), B = (3a₀t₁² + 16v₀t₁ + 30x₀)/(2t₁⁴), C = −(3a₀t₁² + 12v₀t₁ + 20x₀)/(2t₁³), x(t) = At⁵ + Bt⁴ + Ct³ + (a₀/2)t² + v₀t + x₀. (Check: with x₀=1, v₀=a₀=0, t₁=1 this gives 1 − (6t⁵ − 15t⁴ + 10t³), the smootherstep complement.) The supplemental writes the same curve with the signs pulled into x = −At⁵ + Bt⁴ − Ct³ + ...; its text extraction dropped the factor 2 in the denominators, so prefer the slide form. Quintic chosen because it "fits well with the experimentally observed motion of humans [Flash and Hogan 1985]". — [GDC 2018 slides 29, 36](https://media.gdcvault.com/gdc2018/presentations/bollo_david_inertialization_high_performance.pdf); [supplemental eqs. 4–7](https://cdn.gearsofwar.com/thecoalition/publications/SIGGRAPH%202017%20-%20High%20Performance%20Animation%20in%20Gears%20ofWar%204%20-%20Supplemental.pdf)
- Vectors: do not inertialize x, y, z independently ("visual artifacts if vx0, vy0, vz0 are too dissimilar because of transition time clamping"); decompose into direction x̂₀ and magnitude, inertialize the magnitude, with x₋₁ = x⃗₋₁·x⃗₀/|x⃗₀| and v₀ = (x₀ − x₋₁)/Δt, output x⃗(t) = x(t)·x⃗₀/|x⃗₀|. Quaternions: offset q₀ = q_source q_target⁻¹, take its axis and angle, inertialize the angle, with x₋₁ from the twist of q₋₁ about that axis via 2·atan(q_xyz·x̂₀ / q_w) (Shoemake 1994, Graphics Gems IV). — [GDC 2018 slides 38–48](https://media.gdcvault.com/gdc2018/presentations/bollo_david_inertialization_high_performance.pdf)
- Cost: the quintic post-process is 12 µs vs 10 µs for a cubic blend, but total transition cost is 12 µs versus 30 µs (20 µs source evaluation + 10 µs blend) — "60% less"; blend spaces cost 20 µs per animation sequence and 10 µs per blend on Xbox One. — [Bollo 2017 Talk §1–2](https://history.siggraph.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/09/2017-Talks-Bollo_High-Performance-Animation-in-Gears-of-War-4.pdf)
- Foot-phase matching when re-entering locomotion: compute a scalar "phase quotient" from left-foot-relative-to-right-foot position projected on velocity (or forward), negated and offset in the incoming half so it increases monotonically; at startup tabulate it per frame for each locomotion cycle, filter to monotonic, invert to a piecewise-linear map; at runtime start the cycle at the frame matching the current pose's quotient. — [Bollo 2017 Talk §3](https://history.siggraph.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/09/2017-Talks-Bollo_High-Performance-Animation-in-Gears-of-War-4.pdf)
- Design goals listed for the post-process: "Respect the original animation... Smooth and momentum-preserving... No overshoot"; rejected alternatives: output filtering ("difficult to tune, introduces lag, deviates from input even when not transitioning"), blend-from-pose without velocity, velocity match without overshoot control. — [GDC 2018 slides 14–20](https://media.gdcvault.com/gdc2018/presentations/bollo_david_inertialization_high_performance.pdf)

**Daniel Holden's spring-damper toolkit ("Spring-It-On: The Game Developer's Spring-Roll-Call")**
- Exponential damper: `x = lerp(x, g, 1 − 2^(−dt/halflife))`; exact form `damper_exact(x, g, halflife, dt) = lerp(x, g, 1 − fast_negexp((ln2·dt)/(halflife + eps)))`. — [Holden, Spring-Roll-Call](https://theorangeduck.com/page/spring-roll-call)
- Half-life ↔ damping: `halflife_to_damping(h) = 4·ln2/(h + eps)` (≈2.7726/h); frequency ↔ stiffness: `stiffness = (2π f)²`; damping ratio: `stiffness = (damping/(2·ratio))²`, `damping = ratio·2·√stiffness`, ratio 1 = critically damped ("as fast as possible without extra oscillation"). — [Holden, Spring-Roll-Call](https://theorangeduck.com/page/spring-roll-call)
- Exact critically damped spring with goal velocity: d = halflife_to_damping(h); c = g + (d·q)/(d²/4); y = d/2; j0 = x − c; j1 = v + j0·y; e = exp(−y·dt); x ← e·(j0 + j1·dt) + c; v ← e·(v − j1·y·dt). Zero-goal-velocity form: y = d/2, j0 = x − x_goal, j1 = v + j0·y, x ← e(j0 + j1 dt) + x_goal, v ← e(v − j1 y dt). Fast approximation `fast_negexp(x) = 1/(1 + x + 0.48x² + 0.235x³)`. — [Holden, Spring-Roll-Call](https://theorangeduck.com/page/spring-roll-call)
- Spring-based inertialization cost (for motion-matching transition cost): the integral of the decaying offset e^(−yt)(x + (v + xy)t) from 0 to ∞ is (2xy + v)/y²; zero crossing at t = −x/(v + xy); a per-bone feature approximation is (2·pos)/y + vel/y² (y = half-damping from half-life); the cubic-blend analogue is (z·pos)/2 + (z²·vel)/12 with z the blend time; approximations "work well with relatively small half-life" but underestimate cost for oscillations that cross zero. — [Holden, Inertialization Transition Cost](https://theorangeduck.com/page/inertialization-transition-cost)
- Default parameters in Holden's open controller (MIT): `inertialize_blending_halflife = 0.1` s; `simulation_velocity_halflife = 0.27` s and `simulation_rotation_halflife = 0.27` s for the critically damped "simulation object" that turns stick input into desired velocity; run speeds forward/side/back 4.0/3.0/2.5 m/s, walk 1.75/1.5/1.25 m/s; `search_time = 0.1` s; `adjustment_position_halflife = 0.1`, `adjustment_rotation_halflife = 0.2`; `ik_blending_halflife = 0.1`; `gait_change_halflife = 0.1`; a velocity-clamped correction with `max_adjustment_ratio` 0.5 (max correction per frame = ratio × |character velocity| × dt). — [controller.cpp in orangeduck/Motion-Matching](https://github.com/orangeduck/Motion-Matching); [Code vs Data Driven Displacement](https://theorangeduck.com/page/code-vs-data-driven-displacement)

**Verlet particles and spring bones (secondary motion)**
- Thomas Jakobsen, "Advanced Character Physics", GDC 2001 (IO Interactive, Hitman: Codename 47): store x and previous x*, update x′ = 2x − x* + a·Δt² (Verlet); "By lowering the value 2 to something like 1.99 a small amount of drag can also be introduced"; collisions by projecting offending points out of obstacles (zero restitution falls out automatically); distance constraint projection: `delta = x2 − x1; diff = (|delta| − restlength)/|delta|; x1 −= delta·0.5·diff; x2 += delta·0.5·diff`; satisfy all constraints by relaxation (Gauss-Seidel) for NUM_ITERATIONS, adaptively stoppable — "if we stop the iterations early, the result might not end up being quite valid but because of the Verlet scheme, in next frame it will probably be better"; rigid and articulated bodies built from particles plus stick/angular constraints. — [Jakobsen 2001 PDF (CMU mirror)](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/afs/cs/academic/class/15462-s13/www/lec_slides/Jakobsen.pdf)
- Spore's Particle IK reuses Jakobsen's "nonlinear length correction" and per-constraint endpoint "mass" weights (a heavier endpoint moves less), with a C¹ piecewise correction function for soft constraints. — [Hecker et al. 2008, §4.3.1](https://www.chrishecker.com/images/c/cb/Sporeanim-siggraph08.pdf)
- Godot 4.4+ `SpringBoneSimulator3D` (SkeletonModifier3D; "The movement logic is published by VRM Consortium under the MIT license, and SpringBoneSimulator3D reproduces that behavior"; "attempts to return the original pose after modification", unlike PhysicalBoneSimulator3D): per-chain `stiffness` ("the greater the value, the faster it recovers to its initial pose. If stiffness is 0, the modified pose will not return"), `drag` ("the more suppressed the wiggling"), `gravity` ("not an acceleration, but a constant velocity of movement in gravity direction"), `gravity_direction`, `radius` (for `SpringBoneCollision3D`), `rotation_axis` (hinge-like), global `external_force` (Vector3, default 0), `mutable_bone_axes` (default true), optional per-joint overrides and damping Curves along the chain; it "does not factor in twisting forces". — [Godot SpringBoneSimulator3D class reference](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/latest/classes/class_springbonesimulator3d.html); [PR #101409](https://github.com/godotengine/godot/pull/101409)

**Motion warping**
- Witkin & Popović, "Motion Warping", SIGGRAPH '95, pp. 105–108: warp each motion curve θ(t) as θ′(t) = a(t)θ(t) + b(t) with scale a(t) and offset b(t) splines interpolating keyframe constraints (user picks scale or offset per key; a(ti) = (θ′i(ti) − b(ti))/θ(ti) when scaling); time warp t = g(t′) via a Cardinal spline through (t′j, tj) pairs (need not be monotonic); clips are concatenated by overlapping, time-warping to align correspondence points, and blending θblend = w(t)θ1 + (1 − w(t))θ2 with a slow-in/slow-out w; "All of the warped clips we have created required between one and five motion-warping keyframes"; derived from one captured walk: stepping over/onto obstacles, stairs, ducking through a doorway. — [Witkin & Popović 1995 PDF](https://homes.cs.washington.edu/~zoran/warpage/warpage.pdf)
- Production descendants: Gears of War 4 warp points (built on Gleicher 2001 "Motion Path Editing" and Lockwood & Singh 2011) — see Key Question 2. — [Bollo 2017](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3084363.3085069). Unreal Engine's Motion Warping "dynamically adjust[s] a character's root motion to align to targets" via named warp targets and an Anim Notify State window on a montage; modifiers include Skew Warp ("matches the animation's location and rotation in the level at the end of the warping window") and Scale; options: translation/rotation toggles, ignore Z, rotation type (match or face target), rotation speed multiplier. — [Unreal Engine Motion Warping docs](https://dev.epicgames.com/documentation/en-us/unreal-engine/motion-warping-in-unreal-engine)

### Inferences
- Use Bollo's quintic where you need a bounded, guaranteed-overshoot-free blend of a discrete pose switch (e.g. landing pose after a superhuman jump), and Holden's exact critically damped spring where the target itself keeps moving (camera-arm lag, weapon sway, pelvis offset), since the spring needs no explicit end time. Both are ~10 scalar ops per DOF per frame, far below any physics cost.
- With Jolt providing the root "vehicle" (Rosen's model), inertialize in the character's local frame and feed the body's actual acceleration into Rosen's "acceleration tilt" and "impact compression"; the half-life should shrink as strength grows (a stronger character recovers pose faster), which is exactly one parameter in Holden's formulation.
- Godot's `SpringBoneSimulator3D` is sufficient for hair/cloth/holster secondary motion but its "gravity is a velocity" semantics mean it is not a Verlet simulation; for physically consistent secondary motion under superhuman accelerations, feed the skeleton's acceleration into `external_force` or implement Jakobsen's Verlet chain directly.

## Key Question 5: Motion Matching (Clavet 2016), Learned Motion Matching (Holden 2020), and combining motion matching with physics (Ubisoft La Forge)

### Takeaway
Motion matching is a nearest-neighbour search over a ~27-dimensional feature vector (feet positions/velocities, hip velocity, future trajectory at 20/40/60 frames) run every ~10 frames with inertialized transitions; the desired trajectory comes from a critically damped spring "simulation object", which is also the natural coupling point to a physics-driven root. Ubisoft's DReCon turns the kinematic output into PD targets for a Bullet ragdoll (60 Hz, 200 N·m torque clamp) with a small RL policy adding balance corrections; Learned Motion Matching replaces the database with three small networks so memory no longer scales with data.

### Cited Findings

**Motion Matching (Simon Clavet, Ubisoft Montreal, GDC 2016)**
- Talk: "Motion Matching and The Road to Next-Gen Animation", GDC 2016 (For Honor). — [GDC Vault](https://gdcvault.com/play/1023280/Motion-Matching-and-The-Road); [slide text on archive.org](https://archive.org/stream/GDC2016Clavet/GDC2016-Clavet_djvu.txt)
- Algorithm as shown in the slides: "Every frame, look at all mocap and jump at the best place"; a candidate's cost is zero "if the candidate matches the current situation and the piece of motion that follows brings us where we want"; pseudo-code loops over all poses computing `ComputeCost(currentPose, candidatePose, goal)`, keeps the best, and only jumps if the winner is not "at the same location" (same clip and |Δtime| < 0.2 s), then `PlayAnimStartingAtTime(..., 0.25f)` — a 0.25 s blend. — [Clavet 2016 slides](https://archive.org/stream/GDC2016Clavet/GDC2016-Clavet_djvu.txt)
- Features: "Trick 1: Posematch only a few bones" — local velocity, feet positions, feet velocities; "Trick 2: Just check where a piece of animation brings you if you play it" — future position, orientation, velocity/acceleration at `TrajectoryPoint{position, sight, timeDelay}`; cost = current-pose cost + responsivity × future cost. — [Clavet 2016 slides](https://archive.org/stream/GDC2016Clavet/GDC2016-Clavet_djvu.txt)
- Root control: "The code decides the real simulation point"; a "spring damper on velocity: predictable and comfortable"; animations are chosen to follow the simulation and displacement is then taken from animation, with the entity clamped "15 cm around the simulation"; future trajectory predicted by "run[ning] actual simulation code" including "stops on walls and ledges". Optimisations named: LOD, KD-tree, "motion shaders". Workflow: mocap is "tweaked, imported, and marked up", gameplay requests a desired trajectory and event constraints, and "we modify the result to precisely match gameplay and environment" (procedural warping). — [Clavet 2016 slides](https://archive.org/stream/GDC2016Clavet/GDC2016-Clavet_djvu.txt)
- Data volume for navigation: "capture 5 or 10 minutes of a person running around and import it directly into the engine". — [GDC Vault description](https://gdcvault.com/play/1023280/Motion-Matching-and-The-Road)

**Basic motion matching as implemented by Ubisoft La Forge (Holden et al. 2020; Bergamin et al. 2019)**
- Feature vector x ∈ R²⁷ = {future trajectory positions at 20, 40, 60 frames (60 Hz) projected on the ground (R⁶), future facing directions at the same times (R⁶), two foot positions (R⁶), two foot velocities (R⁶), hip velocity (R³)}, each scaled by its dataset standard deviation with a user weight (default 1); search every N ≈ 10 frames or when input changes significantly; k* = argmin‖x̂ − xk‖²; transition with inertialization (Bollo); gaits handled as one-hot features or by restricting the search to tagged ranges; acceleration by KD-tree, voxel lookup, clustering, or a two-level AABB hierarchy over groups of 16 and 64 frames; optional PCA. — [Holden, Kanoun, Perepichka & Popa 2020, Learned Motion Matching, ACM TOG 39(4), §3](https://theorangeduck.com/media/uploads/other_stuff/Learned_Motion_Matching.pdf)
- DReCon's implementation: same 27-D features normalised by mean/std ("not mentioned by Clavet [2016]"); query "every 10 frames, or when the user input changes quickly", "in contrast to Clavet [2016] which suggests matching every frame, which was found to be unnecessarily frequent"; trajectory extrapolated "with a critically damped spring damper, which essentially mixes the current character velocity with the desired user velocity"; inertialization blending "giving the added benefit of only having to evaluate a single track of animation at a time"; style tags via precomputed ranges; KD-tree search. — [Bergamin, Clavet, Holden & Forbes 2019, DReCon, ACM TOG 38(6):206, §5](https://www.theorangeduck.com/media/uploads/other_stuff/DReCon.pdf)
- Holden's open demo weights: foot position 0.75, foot velocity 1.0, hip velocity 1.0, trajectory positions 1.0, trajectory directions 1.5; `search_time` 0.1 s; blend half-life 0.1 s. — [controller.cpp](https://github.com/orangeduck/Motion-Matching)

**Learned Motion Matching (Holden, Kanoun, Perepichka, Popa; SIGGRAPH 2020, ACM TOG 39(4), Article 1, DOI 10.1145/3386569.3392440)**
- Breaks motion matching into Projection (nearest-neighbour search), Stepping (advance index) and Decompression (index → pose), and replaces each with a network: Decompressor (pose from features + latent z ∈ R³²), Stepper (predicts Δx, Δz per frame, trained autoregressively over windows of 2N frames, N ≈ 10), Projector (maps a query to the nearest feature + latent); "Our final model has no need to store animation data or additional matching meta-data in memory"; motivation: basic motion matching's memory "scales linearly with the amount of data". Training: RAdam, batch 32, lr 0.001 decayed ×0.99 per 1000 iterations, up to 500k iterations on a 12-core Xeon CPU ("results in a few hours, training overnight is required for optimal results"). Datasets: locomotion 89,480 frames/27 features; terrain 170,534/35; chair 22,398/19; dog 124,418/39; bear 694,272/55 (all 60 Hz). Terrain adds toe-height features at 0/15/30/45 frames ahead relative to hip height. — [Learned Motion Matching PDF](https://theorangeduck.com/media/uploads/other_stuff/Learned_Motion_Matching.pdf); [project page](https://theorangeduck.com/page/learned-motion-matching); [code](https://github.com/orangeduck/Motion-Matching)

**Motion matching + physics (Ubisoft La Forge)**
- DReCon (SIGGRAPH Asia 2019): kinematic motion-matching controller (10-minute unstructured database: walking, running, strafing, crouching, 60 fps Vicon) → joint angles as open-loop PD targets for a Bullet ragdoll; an RL policy (PPO, 2 hidden layers × 128 tanh units, 110 inputs, 25 outputs) adds corrective offsets on {LeftUpLeg, LeftLeg, LeftFoot, LeftToe, RightUpLeg, RightLeg, RightToe, Spine, LeftArm, RightArm}; policy runs every k = 2 simulation steps (30 Hz) with an exponential action filter y_t = α a_t + (1 − α) y_{t−1}, α = 0.2; simulation 60 Hz, 32 constraint-solver iterations, velocity-constraint PD motors with torque clamp 200 N·m, friction 1.0; character 23 rigid bodies, 54 DOF, 90 kg at 985 kg/m³ from capsule fitting; reward r = e_fall·(r_p + r_v + r_local + r_vCM) with r_p = exp(−10/N·Σ‖p̂ − p‖²) over 6 surface points per body, r_v = exp(−1/N·Σ‖v̂ − v‖²), r_local = exp(−10/N·Σ‖quat diff angle‖), r_vCM = exp(−‖v̂CM − vCM‖²), e_fall = clamp(1.3 − 1.4‖p̂head − phead‖², 0, 1), episode ends when heads are > 1 m apart; training ≈2500 env steps/s on 8 parallel simulations. — [Bergamin et al. 2019, DReCon](https://www.theorangeduck.com/media/uploads/other_stuff/DReCon.pdf); [ACM DOI 10.1145/3355089.3356536](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3355089.3356536)
- SuperTrack (Fussell, Bergamin, Holden; SIGGRAPH Asia 2021): trains a learned world model of the simulator and a policy by supervised back-propagation through it to track kinematic targets, claiming "higher quality of control in a shorter training time" than PPO-based model-free methods. — [SuperTrack project page](https://theorangeduck.com/page/supertrack-motion-tracking-physically-simulated-characters-using-supervised-learning); [Holden publications list](https://theorangeduck.com/page/publications)
- Runtime comparison table in DReCon: DReCon policy 2×128 units, 60 Hz simulation, 30 Hz network query; DeepMimic (Peng et al. 2018) 2 layers of 1024 & 512 units, 1200 Hz simulation, 30 Hz query. — [Bergamin et al. 2019, Table 2](https://www.theorangeduck.com/media/uploads/other_stuff/DReCon.pdf)

### Inferences
- For a Jolt-driven first-person character, the cheapest physics coupling is Clavet's own: let the rigid body be the "simulation object", predict its trajectory with the real integrator (it already handles walls/ledges), query the database against that trajectory, then clamp the visual body within ~15 cm of the physics root and inertialize the residual. A full DReCon-style ragdoll is only warranted if legs/torso must react to contacts.
- Motion matching does not scale to superhuman speeds by itself: the database only contains human-speed clips. Either capture/author exaggerated clips and tag them by strength tier (DReCon-style range restriction), or apply Witkin–Popović time/scale warps (θ′ = aθ + b, time warp g) to human clips as the Froude-based scale factor grows — a hybrid that keeps the procedural path primary.

### Gaps
- Learned Motion Matching's memory/runtime table (its Table 3) was not extracted as text; only the qualitative memory claim is cited.
- Clavet's slides contain no feature weights or database sizes; Holden's open code is the only source of concrete defaults.
- Motion-matching-with-ragdoll production examples beyond La Forge papers (e.g. specific shipped Ubisoft titles) were not verified in this session.

## Key Question 6: First-person rigs — visible arms, legs and torso (camera itself excluded)

### Takeaway
Shipped "true first-person" bodies (Mirror's Edge, DICE) use one full-body skeleton with the camera on the head bone and the head mesh hidden, spine/neck driven by pitch/yaw aim offsets, and first-person animations authored for the view rather than for third-person plausibility; Rosen's indie approach adds spring-based weapon recoil and interpolated hand poses, and Godot's spring bones and two-bone IK cover the same needs.

### Cited Findings
- Mirror's Edge (DICE): "Creating First Person Movement for MIRROR'S EDGE", Tobias Dahl and Mikael Lagre, GDC China 2009, on "a believable first person full body experience", the animation tools/pipeline and how moves evolved from prototype to final. — [GDC Vault](https://gdcvault.com/play/1012172/Creating-First-Person-Movement-for). A summary notes DICE tried "attaching a camera to a mocapped head, though it didn't work out and good old animator talent was the solution". — [Game Anim summary](https://www.gameanim.com/2010/11/05/creating-first-person-movement-for-mirrors-edge/)
- First-person animation principles (Ryan Duffin, EA/Danger Close — Battlefield 3, Medal of Honor: Warfighter — GDC 2013 Animation Bootcamp, as summarised by David Rosen): first-person animations viewed in third person "look bizarre, with very little center-of-mass movement, and cartoony, spider-like arm movements", and third-person-style animation fails in first person; first-person motion works like a HUD, conveying weapon type, power, readiness and "pain, strain, exertion and weight"; timing variations (ADS speed, reload time, movement multipliers) differentiate weapons; camera movement "adds punctuation to forceful actions but must be used carefully to avoid motion sickness"; weapons were composed on screen along a golden-ratio spiral. — [Wolfire blog, GDC13 Summary: Animation Bootcamp Part 5/6](https://www.wolfire.com/blog/2013/04/GDC13-Summary-Animation-Bootcamp-Part-5-6/)
- True first-person full-body implementation (Fabrice Piquet, 21 June 2018, UE4): one skeletal mesh for the whole body with the camera attached to the head socket so camera motion comes from the skeleton; head mesh hidden for the owner; spine and neck driven by Aim Offsets ("additive animation (1 frame animation) that will be used as offsets") blending 10 directional poses by pitch and yaw so the body follows the view; a one-frame lag is avoided by updating the camera from the PlayerController tick before the mesh evaluates; motion sickness reduced by "a constraint on the character head to always look at a specific point" far away during looping locomotion; full-body montages (landing) override the aim offset, disable input and reset pitch to avoid double rotation. — [Game Developer, True First Person Camera in Unreal Engine 4](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/programming/true-first-person-camera-in-unreal-engine-4)
- Rosen's first-person section (Receiver): "Linear interpolation" of hand/weapon poses, a "Recoil spring", a "Flashlight — what needs to be lit? how many hands?" case, and "Death is important — random variation"; his SOTC case study lists "2-part spring pendulum, IK + rotation". — [Rosen 2014 slides 42–46, 65–66](https://archive.org/download/GDC2014Rosen/GDC2014-Rosen_djvu.xml)
- Solver sizing for first-person limbs (secondary source): two-bone solvers suffice for foot placement and simple hand reach. — [StraySpark UE5.7 guide (search snippet)](https://www.strayspark.studio/blog/procedural-animation-ik-ue57-guide)
- Godot building blocks available in 4.6+: `TwoBoneIK3D` with pole targets for arms/legs, `LookAtModifier3D`-style aim (via the `SkeletonModifier3D` family) and `SpringBoneSimulator3D` with `external_force` for weapon/holster sway. — [Godot TwoBoneIK3D](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/latest/classes/class_twoboneik3d.html); [Godot SpringBoneSimulator3D](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/latest/classes/class_springbonesimulator3d.html)

### Inferences
- Because the player only ever sees arms, upper chest and (when looking down) legs, the "consequence" cues for superhuman power must be carried by those parts: Duffin's HUD principle says exaggerate arm strain/settle time and weapon spring parameters as strength grows; Piquet's aim-offset spine is where torque from a superhuman turn should show as lag (a Holden spring on yaw with half-life shrinking with strength).
- Rosen's "recoil spring" and Holden's exact critically damped spring are the same object; the recoil impulse should be the physics impulse (mass × velocity change) so weapon kick scales correctly with the character's growing strength.

### Gaps
- No text of the Mirror's Edge talk itself was accessible; details are from the Vault abstract and a secondary summary.
- No primary source on the biomechanics of a first-person visible legs rig (foot visibility, knee-lift exaggeration) was found; Piquet's article covers the head/camera coupling more than the legs.
