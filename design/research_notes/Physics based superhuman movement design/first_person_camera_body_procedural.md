## Key question 1: How do shipped first-person games with visible bodies animate the camera?

### Takeaway
Shipped games fall into two camps: (a) camera rigidly parented to an animated head bone with the animation itself authored to be comfortable (Mirror's Edge, Dying Light, Star Citizen, GTA V), and (b) a stable, input-driven camera with small additive, decaying offsets (Quake/Half-Life "punch angle" spring, Titanfall's landing spring and wall-run tilt). Every studio that spoke about it reports the same failure mode: the raw head-bone camera is a "bobble head" that causes sickness, and the fix is either hand-animated camera restraint, a counter-translating stabiliser, or exposing toggles to the player.

- Reviewer experience: "I could get my arms or body stuck between two pipes ... ### Inferences
- Across DICE, Techland, Respawn and CIG the common rule is: rotation of the camera must be player-driven; body-driven *translation* is tolerated only when small, predictable and smoothed. This maps directly onto the Oculus rule "User and camera movements should never be decoupled" (see KQ3), even for flat screens.
- For a physics-driven body, CIG's "bird neck" is the most directly reusable idea: treat the camera as a stabilised end-effector whose target is the head but whose *world-space* position is filtered, and let the neck/body absorb the difference (Star Citizen does this in animation; in a Jolt rig it becomes a soft constraint between the simulated head and a filtered camera anchor).
- Every game that ships a body-driven camera also ships an opt-out (GTA V: three separate toggles; Cyberpunk: "Additive camera motion"; DOOM: head bob toggle per player reports in KQ3). Plan the toggles from the start and keep the "additive" layer separable from the base view.

## Key question 2: Camera math — bob, roll, punch, trauma shake, spring-damper lag, foot-contact-driven bob

### Takeaway
The lineage Quake → Half-Life → Source gives fully reproducible formulas: a velocity-scaled sine bob with a 30%/70% DC/AC split clamped to [-7, +4] units, a lateral-velocity view roll saturating at `rollangle` degrees for lateral speeds ≥ `rollspeed`, a landing roll of `fallSpeed × 0.013` degrees with pitch capped at 8°, and a damped torsional spring for "punch" (k = 65, c = 9). Eiserloh's trauma model (`shake = trauma^2` or `^3`, Perlin noise per axis, rotation-only in 3D) and Holden's exact critically damped spring (half-life parameterised, lag = halflife/ln 2) supply the smoothing and impact layers. Commercial FP controllers (Opsive) already gate bob on ground contact and add a "trough force" at the low point, which is the hook for a foot-contact-driven bob.

### Cited Findings

**Quake (id Software, 1996) — origin of the formulas**
- Defaults: `cl_rollspeed 200`, `cl_rollangle 2.0`, `cl_bob 0.02`, `cl_bobcycle 0.6`, `cl_bobup 0.5`, `v_kicktime 0.5`, `v_kickroll 0.6`, `v_kickpitch 0.6`, idle sway `v_iyaw_cycle 2`, `v_iroll_cycle 0.5`, `v_ipitch_cycle 1`, `v_iyaw_level 0.3`, `v_iroll_level 0.1`, `v_ipitch_level 0.3`, `v_idlescale 0` (idle sway off by default). — [WinQuake/view.c, id-Software/Quake on GitHub](https://github.com/id-Software/Quake/blob/master/WinQuake/view.c)
- `V_CalcBob`: `cycle = fmod(time, cl_bobcycle)/cl_bobcycle`; if `cycle < cl_bobup` then `cycle = π·cycle/cl_bobup` else `cycle = π + π·(cycle−cl_bobup)/(1−cl_bobup)`; `bob = |v_xy|·cl_bob`; `bob = bob·0.3 + bob·0.7·sin(cycle)`; clamp to `[-7, 4]`. Comment: "bob is proportional to velocity in the xy plane (don't count Z, or jumping messes it up)". — [view.c](https://github.com/id-Software/Quake/blob/master/WinQuake/view.c)
- Damage kick: `v_dmg_roll = count·(from·right)·v_kickroll`; `v_dmg_pitch = count·(from·forward)·v_kickpitch`; `v_dmg_time = v_kicktime`; each frame `viewangles[ROLL] += v_dmg_time/v_kicktime·v_dmg_roll` (same for pitch), `v_dmg_time −= frametime` — a linear ramp-down over 0.5 s. Dead view: `viewangles[ROLL] = 80`. — [view.c](https://github.com/id-Software/Quake/blob/master/WinQuake/view.c)

**Half-Life 1 SDK (Valve, cl_dll/view.cpp)**
- Registered defaults: `cl_bobcycle "0.8"`, `cl_bob "0.01"`, `cl_bobup "0.5"` (comment: "best default for my experimental gun wag (sjb)"). `V_CalcBob` is the Quake formula but advances `bobtime += frametime` only when `onground != -1`, and returns the old value otherwise. `V_CalcRoll` is identical to Quake; `V_CalcViewRoll` uses `movevars->rollangle/rollspeed`. — [cl_dll/view.cpp, ValveSoftware/halflife](https://github.com/ValveSoftware/halflife/blob/master/cl_dll/view.cpp)
- Client-side punch decay `V_DropPunchAngle`: `len = |punch|; len −= (10.0 + len·0.5)·frametime; len = max(len, 0)`; server punch and client punch are both added to view angles. — [view.cpp](https://github.com/ValveSoftware/halflife/blob/master/cl_dll/view.cpp)
- Idle sway (`V_AddIdle`): `viewangles[ROLL] += v_idlescale·sin(t·v_iroll_cycle)·v_iroll_level` etc.; viewmodel pitch uses `v_ipitch_level·0.5` "to prevent normally unseen parts of viewmodel from coming into view". — [view.cpp](https://github.com/ValveSoftware/halflife/blob/master/cl_dll/view.cpp)

**Squirrel Eiserloh, "Math for Game Programmers: Juicing Your Cameras With Math", GDC 2016**
- "Maintain a 'trauma' level in [0,1]"; "Damage, stress adds trauma (+= 0.2 or 0.5)"; "Trauma decreases (linearly) over time"; "Camera shake is trauma² or trauma³"; "Trauma .30, .60, .90 means 3%, 22%, 73% shake" (cubic). — [Full transcript, archive.org](https://archive.org/stream/GDC2016Eiserloh/GDC2016-Eiserloh_djvu.txt)
- 3D shake: `yaw = maxYaw·shake·GetPerlinNoise(seed, time)`, `pitch = maxPitch·shake·GetPerlinNoise(seed+1, time)`, `roll = maxRoll·shake·GetPerlinNoise(seed+2, time)`; 2D uses translational + rotational ("Awesome"); in 3D "Rotational: nice!" while translational shake is "super lame"/"VERY BAD"; "Tread carefully in VR!" — [transcript](https://archive.org/stream/GDC2016Eiserloh/GDC2016-Eiserloh_djvu.txt)
- Why noise: "Smoothed noise feels better... automagically works with pause and slow-motion... has adjustable frequency... more easily reproducible on replay." — [transcript](https://archive.org/stream/GDC2016Eiserloh/GDC2016-Eiserloh_djvu.txt)
- Asymptotic averaging: `x += (target − x)·0.1` ("Each frame we move 10% closer"); weights "0.01 = nice and slow (at 60 FPS)", "0.1 = reasonably fast", "0.5 = incredibly fast"; scale by `timeScale`; "Nothing says horizontal and vertical camera motion need to be designed the same. Nor does upward movement need to be governed by the same rules as downward movement." — [transcript](https://archive.org/stream/GDC2016Eiserloh/GDC2016-Eiserloh_djvu.txt)
- The talk gives no numeric maximum roll or offset. — [transcript](https://archive.org/stream/GDC2016Eiserloh/GDC2016-Eiserloh_djvu.txt); slides PDF (503 at access time): [mathforgameprogrammers.com](http://www.mathforgameprogrammers.com/gdc2016/GDC2016_Eiserloh_Squirrel_JuicingYourCameras.pdf)
- A widely used Unity implementation of the talk: `traumaExponent = 2`, `recoverySpeed = 1.5` (trauma units/s), noise `frequency = 25`, `Maximum Angular Shake = (15, 15, 10)` degrees, `Maximum Translation Shake = (1, 1, 1)` units, distance falloff `stress = (1 − clamp01(d/range)²)·maximumStress` with `range = 45`. — [Unity Camera Shake Tutorial, Roystan](https://roystan.net/articles/camera-shake/)

**Daniel Holden, "Spring-It-On: The Game Developer's Spring-Roll-Call" (theorangeduck.com)**
- Exact damper: `damper_exact(x, g, halflife, dt) = lerp(x, g, 1 − exp(−(ln2·dt)/(halflife+eps)))`; `halflife_to_damping(halflife) = 4·ln2/(halflife+eps)`. — [Spring-It-On](https://theorangeduck.com/page/spring-roll-call)
- Critically damped spring, exact: `y = halflife_to_damping(halflife)/2; j0 = x − x_goal; j1 = v + j0·y; eydt = exp(−y·dt); x = eydt·(j0 + j1·dt) + x_goal; v = eydt·(v − j1·y·dt)`. — [Spring-It-On](https://theorangeduck.com/page/spring-roll-call)
- Tracking lag: "the amount of time the spring will lag behind a target... is halflife / ln(2)" (`halflife_to_lag`), which motivates predicting the target ahead (velocity spring) or inertialization (`inertialize_update`: decay an offset spring and add it to the new input) to avoid "discontinuities" when switching animations. The article gives no camera-specific halflife recommendations. — [Spring-It-On](https://theorangeduck.com/page/spring-roll-call)
- Alexis Bacot ("The Art of Damping") adds that Unity's SmoothDamp is "critically damped, meaning it returns to the goal as fast as possible without extra oscillation" and "frame rate independent", and that "There is no winning damping function, they're all useful in certain situations"; it cites Game Programming Gems 4 (Thomas Lowe) for the critically damped spring. — [The Art of Damping](https://www.alexisbacot.com/blog/the-art-of-damping)

**Spring-based FP camera systems in shipped middleware (Opsive Ultimate Character Controller)**
- Spring parameters: Stiffness (V3 uses `1 − Stiffness`, so "lower value produces stronger return"), Damping ("Multiplies the remaining velocity on every update"), Velocity Fade In Length, Max Soft Force Frames, Min Velocity (snap-to-rest), Max Velocity ("Caps the magnitude of the spring velocity so a large force cannot produce an unlimited response"), Min/Max Value ("Clamp the resulting position or rotation on each axis. Use them as safety limits, not as the main way to shape the motion"). Forces are "soft" (distributed over frames) or instant; Position/Rotation Fall Impact have Softness fields. — [Opsive: Springs](https://opsive.com/support/documentation/ultimate-character-controller/animation/springs/)
- First-person view type: bob "Positional and roll rates set frequency; amplitudes set strength"; "Trough offset and force add a reaction at the low point. Bob Require Ground Contact prevents normal bob while airborne"; "Position and rotation fall-impact values react to landings, while Rotation Strafe Roll leans with sideways velocity"; secondary springs "return short forces such as recoil"; "Smooth Head Offset Steps averages recent humanoid head offsets. Set it to 0 to disable head-position tracking"; "Rotate With Head includes animated head rotation"; tuning advice: "Minimize springs, bob, and shake first, then enable one source at a time." — [Opsive: First Person view type](https://opsive.com/support/documentation/ultimate-character-controller/camera/view-types/included-view-types/first-person/)

**Godot-specific example**
- A Godot forum solution drives bob by velocity: `bobOscillate = sin(time * velMag * (2*PI))`, adds it to the pivot's vertical basis, and clamps roll `rotation_degrees.z = clamp(bobOscillate*5, -4, 4)`. — [Godot Forum: head bobbing oscillator in GDScript](https://forum.godotengine.org/t/how-does-one-program-a-head-bobbing-oscillator-in-gdscript/10121)

### Inferences
- Source's punch spring, written as `θ̈ = −65·θ − 9·θ̇`, has ω₀ = √65 ≈ 8.06 rad/s (≈1.28 Hz, natural period ≈0.78 s) and damping ratio ζ = 9/(2·8.06) ≈ 0.56: deliberately underdamped so a hit overshoots once and settles in roughly half a second. Valve's ±89°/±179° clamps are only anti-wrap guards; the shipped landing effect never exceeds ~12° (922.5 u/s × 0.013) and pitch is hard-capped at 8°. Those are usable as the roll/pitch ceiling for an impact reaction on a flat screen.
- Converting HL2 units at the commonly cited 1 unit = 0.75 in (Valve Developer Community "Dimensions", not fetched here): the punch threshold 303 u/s ≈ 5.8 m/s (≈1.7 m drop) gives ≈3.9° roll; the max-safe 526.5 u/s ≈ 10 m/s (≈5.1 m drop) gives ≈6.8° roll; roll therefore scales ≈0.68° per m/s of impact speed. This is a ready-made mapping from Jolt's measured landing velocity to camera roll for the "hard landing" consequence.
- Both HL1 (`onground` gate) and Opsive ("Bob Require Ground Contact") stop advancing bob in the air, while HL2 deliberately lets the phase continue "because it snaps badly without it": the lesson is that whatever drives bob must be continuous at take-off and landing — a spring excited by contact impulses satisfies this automatically, a sine gated on ground does not.
- A foot-contact-driven bob is the natural combination of Titanfall's "spring back proportional to fall height", Opsive's "trough force" and Holden's exact critically damped spring: on each foot-contact event from the physics sim, add a downward velocity impulse to a vertical camera spring proportional to the foot's vertical impact speed (and a lateral impulse signed by which foot), with halflife ≈ 0.1–0.2 s so the lag (halflife/ln2 ≈ 0.14–0.29 s) is shorter than a step. This keeps bob energy at gait frequency (≈1.5–2.5 Hz for walking/running cadence, well above the sickness band in KQ3) and makes superhuman running produce larger impulses without a hand-tuned amplitude curve.
- Because Quake/HL1/HL2 default `v_idlescale 0` and HL2 default `sv_rollangle 0`, the historical trend at Valve is toward *less* camera motion, with all expressive motion moved to the viewmodel/arms. This supports putting most of the "body" feel into the arms/weapon layer rather than the camera transform.

### Gaps
- Eiserloh's slide PDF (with any numeric max-roll guidance) was unavailable; the transcript contains no roll/offset ceilings.
- No developer-published parameter values for Titanfall's landing spring or wall-run tilt angle were found (only qualitative statements).
- Quake's `view.c` header comment states "The view is allowed to move slightly from it's true position for bobbing" ([view.c line 27](https://github.com/id-Software/Quake/blob/master/WinQuake/view.c)), confirming the bob offsets the view origin in Quake, but the exact line applying `bob` to `r_refdef.vieworg` was not extracted; the HL1/HL2 successors apply bob mainly to the viewmodel.

## Key question 3: Comfort and sickness — vection, acceleration, frequency, FOV, roll, forced rotation, prevalence and mitigations

### Takeaway
The Oculus/Meta guidance is explicit and numeric: avoid visually presented acceleration (any change of velocity, including turning, stopping and bobbing), keep speeds near 1.4 m/s walking / 2.8–3 m/s running, avoid vertical oscillation near 0.2 Hz and off-vertical rotation near 0.3 Hz, do not head-bob, never decouple camera from user input, and let the player lower intensity. Laboratory work pins the worst band for visually induced sickness at 0.2–0.4 Hz for fore-aft oscillation (and 0.2 Hz for physical translation). Flat-screen prevalence is not negligible: 42–56% of students got sick within 50 min of console games in one controlled study, and 2.5–5% of Steam reviews of VR FPS games mention sickness.

### Cited Findings

**Oculus Best Practices (Oculus VR, LLC, Version 310-30000-02, © 2017)** — [PDF](https://static.oculus.com/documentation/pdfs/intro-vr/latest/bp.pdf)
- "Acceleration creates a mismatch among your visual, vestibular, and proprioceptive senses. Minimize the duration and frequency of such conflicts. Make accelerations as short (preferably instantaneous) and infrequent as you can."
- "'acceleration' ... refers to any change in the motion of the user, whether in direction or speed. Slowing down or stopping, turning while moving or standing still, and stepping or getting pushed sideways are all forms of acceleration."
- "Have accelerations initiated and controlled by the user whenever possible. Shaking, jerking, or bobbing the camera will be uncomfortable for the player."
- "users are most comfortable moving through virtual environments at a constant velocity. Real-world speeds will be comfortable for longer. For reference, humans walk at an average rate of 1.4 m/s." Elsewhere: "about 1.4 m/s walking, 3 m/s for a continuous jogging pace" as a user-configurable option.
- "Avoid vertical linear oscillations, which are most discomforting at 0.2 Hz, and off-vertical-axis rotations, which are most discomforting at 0.3 Hz."
- "Do not use 'head bobbing' camera effects. They create a series of small but uncomfortable accelerations." / "Don't use head bobbing in first-person games." / "Every bob up and down is another bit of acceleration applied to the user's view ... Do not use any head-bob or changes in orientation or position of the camera that were not initiated by the real-world motion of the user's head."
- "User and camera movements should never be decoupled." / "avoid jerking the camera around, such as when the user is hit or shot. This can be very effective on a monitor, but can cause simulator sickness."
- "Instantaneous accelerations are more comfortable than gradual accelerations ... discomfort will increase as a function of the frequency, size, and duration of acceleration."
- "Research suggests that providing users with an avatar that anticipates and foreshadows the visual motion they are about to experience allows them to prepare for it in a way that reduces discomfort."
- "Left or right lateral movement is more problematic because we don't normally walk sideways"; stairs/slopes and streaming stripes "drive a strong sense of vection"; a 3-DOF control scheme produced less sickness than 6-DOF in an obstacle-course study "because it saved them from having to experience extraneous visual motion."
- "Consider implementing mechanisms that allow users to adjust the intensity ... movement speed, the size of accelerations, or the breadth of the displayed FOV. Any such settings should default to the lowest-intensity experience." and allow changing them "in-game without restarting".
- "visually induced motion sickness occurs more readily in virtual reality headsets than with computer or TV screens."
- FOV: "A wide dFOV is more likely to contribute to simulator sickness" (motion perception is more sensitive in the periphery); "Reducing display FOV can reduce simulator sickness"; changing camera FOV so "a 10° rotation of the head creates a rotation of the virtual world that would normally require a 15° rotation" causes VOR gain adaptation. Zoom effects are advised against.
- Vection is cited to Hettinger, Berbaum, Kennedy, Dunlap & Nolan (1990), "Vection and simulator sickness", Military Psychology 2(3), 171–181.

**Meta Horizon OS locomotion guidance (current web pages)**
- Quantized velocity: "setting fixed movement speeds (for example, stopped, walking, running) and switching between them instantly"; speeds "walking ~3 mph/1.4 m/s", "running ~6 mph/2.8 m/s"; snap-turn angles "like 15, 30, 45, 90, or 180 degrees"; "soft collisions" where the "camera slows down before stopping, rather than an abrupt halt"; keep camera at "a consistent elevation relative to the ground"; vignettes that "darken or occlude screen edges during movement, limiting visible optic flow"; "Users experience less discomfort when they can predict camera movements." — [Locomotion comfort and usability, Meta Horizon OS Developers](https://developers.meta.com/horizon/design/locomotion-comfort-usability/)
- "Keep acceleration events brief and infrequent"; match avatar speeds to real-world walking and running. — [Locomotion Best Practices, Meta](https://developers.meta.com/horizon/design/locomotion-best-practices/)

**Frequency of provocative motion (laboratory)**
- Diels & Howarth (Human Factors, 2013): 24 participants, random-dot optic flow simulating fore-aft oscillation from 0.025 to 1.6 Hz, 20-min exposures; "VIMS peaked between 0.2 and 0.4 Hz with a reducing effect at lower and higher frequencies"; application: "frequencies around 0.2 to 0.4 Hz should be avoided." — [Frequency characteristics of visually induced motion sickness, doi:10.1177/0018720812469046 (abstract via Europe PMC)](https://doi.org/10.1177/0018720812469046)
- Golding, Mueller & Gresty (2001): physical horizontal fore-aft oscillation at 1.0 m/s² peak, 0.1/0.2/0.4 Hz; moderate nausea in 8/12, 12/12, 7/12 subjects respectively; time to endpoint 18.0 / 11.2 / 20.2 min; "A maximum nauseogenic potential around 0.2 Hz was substantiated." — [A motion sickness maximum around the 0.2 Hz frequency range (abstract via Europe PMC)](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=%22motion%20sickness%20maximum%20around%20the%200.2%20Hz%20frequency%22&format=json&resultType=core)
- Duh, Parker, Philips & Furness (Human Factors, 2004): conflicting visual/vestibular cues at the "crossover" frequency of about 0.06 Hz were more likely to evoke simulator sickness than at a higher frequency (p < .01), giving a "preliminary design guidance curve". — [doi:10.1518/hfes.46.1.142.30384](https://doi.org/10.1518/hfes.46.1.142.30384)

**Field of view (flat screen)**
- de Vries, Bos, van Emmerik & Groen (TNO, VIMS2007 proceedings): internal FOV (IFOV, "the FOV which the graphics generator is using to render its images") vs external FOV (EFOV, "the FOV of the presented images as seen from the physical viewpoint of the observer"); vertical EFOV 30° (203 cm) or 60° (58 cm), horizontal EFOV 49.6° and 103.1°; IFOV set to 30° or 60°; "Games usually have a vertical IFOV in the order of 75-90º". Result: the *congruent* conditions produced the highest sickness (MaxMISC, F(3,35)=12.3, p<.0001); "incongruent internal and external FOVs (conditions 2 and 3) led to lower average levels of sickness"; they predict that ever-larger displays "will steadily approach the sweet spot of the EFOV/IFOV curve that will cause maximal cybersickness." Note the MSSQ mean of 21.5 indicated below-average susceptibility. — [Internal and external Field of View: computer games and cybersickness, VIMS2007 pp. 89–95](https://www.ieda.ust.hk/dfaculty/so/pdf/Pages89-95-VIMS2007.pdf)
- Oculus (above) recommends user-adjustable FOV and treats wide display FOV as a risk factor while warning against camera-FOV manipulation that breaks head-rotation gain. — [Oculus Best Practices](https://static.oculus.com/documentation/pdfs/intro-vr/latest/bp.pdf)

**Prevalence on flat screens and in FPS games**
- Stoffregen, Faugloire, Yoshida, Flanagan & Merhi (Human Factors, 2008): 40 undergraduates played off-the-shelf console games up to 50 min, standing or sitting, at varied screen distance; "the incidence of motion sickness ranged from 42% to 56%; incidence did not differ across conditions"; "console video games carry a significant risk of motion sickness." — [doi:10.1518/001872008x250755](https://doi.org/10.1518/001872008x250755)
- Chang, Pan, Chen & Stoffregen (Exp Brain Res, 2013): 24 adults, 50 min of a driving game while passively restrained; "Five of 24 participants (20.83 %) reported motion sickness." — [doi:10.1007/s00221-013-3609-y](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00221-013-3609-y)
- Merhi, Faugloire, Flanagan & Stoffregen (Human Factors, 2007): commercial Xbox games via HMD, up to 50 min; "Sickness occurred in all conditions, but it was more common during standing." — [doi:10.1518/001872007x230262](https://doi.org/10.1518/001872007x230262)
- Guzsvinecz, Nemeth & Szűcs (Virtual Reality, 2026): 1,375,125 English Steam reviews of VR FPS games, 1,898 cybersickness-focused; cybersickness mentioned in 2.46% of positive and 5.02% of negative reviews; complaints name smooth locomotion and "acceleration and deceleration when you touch or release movement control"; coping strategies: session control 27.9%, seated play 23.6%, locomotion adjustment 17.8%, controller swapping 12.3%, settings (disabling motion blur, modifying FOV) 10.1%, habituation 4.7%. — [Cybersickness in commercial first-person virtual reality video games: analysis of player experience in steam reviews](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10055-026-01353-y)

**Player-reported mitigations on flat screens**
- "The main thing that has the potential to make me sick is head/camera bob...Devs, please offer a way to disable involuntary camera movement" (JudgmentJay, 2017-11-14); "DOOM still bugs me if I play it even now, but turning off headbob makes it feel a lot better" (Rirse, 2019-06-29); "Bad motion blur makes motion sickness much worse for me, but good motion blur can help sometimes" and FOV options "are often too limited" (Pargon, 2017-10-27). — [ResetEra: First Person and motion sickness](https://www.resetera.com/threads/first-person-and-motion-sickness.295178/page-2)
- Practitioner checklist (anecdotal, author's own words: "I don't have any evidence other than anecdotal"): disable motion blur first; stabilise then raise frame rate (one person sick at 30 FPS, "no issues at 144 FPS"); raise FOV; disable depth of field, vignetting, chromatic aberration; disable head sway/bob where offered (GTA V cited); reduce ADS zoom; bright dot reticle. — [Alleviating motion sickness in first-person video games, Nicolas Busseneau, 2020-09](https://nicolas.busseneau.fr/en/blog/2020/09/alleviating-motion-sickness-in-first-person-video-games)
- Cyberpunk 2077 players: disabling "Additive camera motion" removes head bob but "does not 100% remove" sickness for some. — [Steam thread, Dec 2020](https://steamcommunity.com/app/1091500/discussions/0/2988665684326677514/)

### Inferences
- The Oculus 0.2 Hz / 0.3 Hz numbers and the Diels & Howarth 0.2–0.4 Hz band are the same phenomenon; for a flat-screen design the actionable rule is: any slow camera drift, idle sway, breathing sway or "inertia" lag whose energy lies between ~0.1 and ~0.5 Hz is the most provocative kind of motion, while gait-rate bob (1.5–2.5 Hz) and short impact springs (>1 Hz, as in Source's 1.28 Hz punch spring) sit above the band. This argues against long camera-lag half-lives (>0.3 s) for body-following: they turn every stop/start into a ~0.3–1 s oscillation in the danger band. (Note the mocaponline guide's suggested breathing sway at "12–18 cycles per minute (~0.2–0.3 Hz)" sits exactly in that band; keep breathing sway on the arms, not the camera.)
- "Acceleration" in the Oculus sense includes rotation, so a physics body that rotates the camera (stumbles, ragdoll) is the highest-risk element of the whole design; Titanfall's rule "any time we move the view without your input that can cause motion sickness" is the flat-screen version of the same finding. Body-induced rotation should be (a) opt-in, (b) clamped to the HL2 landing ceiling (≈8–12°), (c) short (decays within ~0.5–1 s), and (d) foreshadowed by the visible body (Oculus: avatar that "anticipates and foreshadows the visual motion").
- The de Vries/Bos result complicates the folk rule "match rendered FOV to display FOV": in their data the *matched* case was the sickest. Practical reading: expose a wide FOV range (players in the ResetEra thread complain about ranges being too narrow), default near 90° vertical-equivalent-aware value, and do not tie head-bob amplitude to FOV.
- No source gives a fraction of *flat-screen* players made sick specifically by head bob; the best available are the 42–56% console-game incidence (50 min, Stoffregen 2008) and the 2.5–5% of VR FPS reviews mentioning sickness. Treat head-bob sensitivity as common enough that the default should be the low-intensity setting (Oculus: "default to the lowest-intensity experience").

### Gaps
- No Valve/SteamVR written comfort guideline document was retrievable (only third-party summaries); Half-Life: Alyx's own comfort options were not sourced.
- The claim from search snippets that a 2020 Ergonomics in Design study found FOV < 80° increased nausea and > 110° increased discomfort could not be verified against the paper; treat as unconfirmed.
- No study isolating head-bob amplitude/frequency thresholds on flat screens was found (Europe PMC query for "head bob" cybersickness returned nothing relevant).
- Roll tolerance limits (degrees of camera roll acceptable on flat screens) are not quantified in any source found; only shipped values (Quake 2°, Godot example ±4°, HL2 landing ≤12° transient, HL1 dead-cam 80°).

## Key question 4: How should the camera react to a physics impact (wall at speed, hard landing)? Rigid head versus smoothed follow target; decoupling with limits

### Takeaway
No shipped game in the sources binds the camera rigidly to a simulated head. The evidence-backed pattern is a three-layer stack: (1) a player-rotated base view; (2) a filtered follow of the head *position* with counter-translation absorbed by the body (Star Citizen's bird-neck stabiliser) and small clamps (Opsive Min/Max Value); (3) additive, decaying impulse reactions for impacts (Quake/Source punch spring, Titanfall landing spring, HL2 fall-speed-proportional roll with an 8° pitch cap). When the head is pushed into geometry, VR practice is to fade the view rather than move the camera.

### Cited Findings
- Star Citizen: raw head-mounted camera = "bobble head"; eye counter-rotation removed ~80% of the bounce but caused sickness in confined spaces and when strafing near walls; final solution counter-translates the body so the head/camera stays steady ("a camera stabilizer invented by nature"). — [Kotaku, 2016](https://kotaku.com/star-citizens-first-person-view-is-based-on-birds-1787021141)
- Titanfall: landing reaction implemented purely as a camera spring "with intensity proportional to fall height" on top of unchanged movement code; view moved without input causes sickness. — [Game Developer, 2014](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/designer-interview-getting-i-titanfall-i-s-controls-just-right)
- Source: landing roll `= fallSpeed × 0.013` deg, pitch clamp 8°, only above a threshold fall speed (303 u/s HL2, "at least a 76" fall"); general impacts via `ViewPunch` → velocity impulse `×20` into a spring with k=65, c=9. — [gamemovement.cpp](https://github.com/ValveSoftware/source-sdk-2013/blob/master/src/game/shared/gamemovement.cpp); [baseplayer_shared.cpp](https://github.com/ValveSoftware/source-sdk-2013/blob/master/src/game/shared/baseplayer_shared.cpp)
- Quake damage kick: direction-dependent roll/pitch (`side`/`forward` dot products with the hit direction × 0.6 per damage count), linear decay over 0.5 s. — [view.c](https://github.com/id-Software/Quake/blob/master/WinQuake/view.c)
- Oculus: "avoid jerking the camera around, such as when the user is hit or shot. This can be very effective on a monitor, but can cause simulator sickness"; "In general, avoid decoupling the user's and camera's movements for any reason." — [Oculus Best Practices](https://static.oculus.com/documentation/pdfs/intro-vr/latest/bp.pdf)
- Meta: "soft collisions" — "camera slows down before stopping, rather than an abrupt halt"; keep camera at consistent elevation over terrain. — [Meta locomotion comfort](https://developers.meta.com/horizon/design/locomotion-comfort-usability/)
- Boneworks: whole avatar is a colliding physical object; body is "obstructed by objects"; reviewers report the body getting stuck and long sessions causing sickness; SLZ recommends prior VR experience. — [Road to VR 2019](https://roadtovr.com/boneworks-reveal-stress-level-zero-physics-based-interactions-embodiment/); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boneworks); [Road to VR overview](https://roadtovr.com/boneworks-physics-driven-mechanics-gameplay-overview/); [Steam](https://store.steampowered.com/app/823500/BONEWORKS/)
- Standard VR head-collision handling (VRTK `HeadsetCollisionFade`): when the headset collides with geometry the script "fades the screen to a solid colour"; rationale: "if the user puts their head where it shouldn't be, then fading to a colour (e.g. black) will make the user realise they've done something wrong and they'll probably naturally step backwards"; parameters Blink Transition Speed, Fade Color, Ignore Target With Tag Or Class. — [VRTK docs](https://vrtoolkit.readme.io/v2.2.1/docs/vrtk_headsetcollisionfade)
- Opsive: springs have Max Velocity ("a large force cannot produce an unlimited response") and Min/Max Value clamps ("Use them as safety limits"); separate Position/Rotation Fall Impact springs with Softness; "Smooth Head Offset Steps averages recent humanoid head offsets" for body-driven head tracking. — [Opsive Springs](https://opsive.com/support/documentation/ultimate-character-controller/animation/springs/); [Opsive First Person](https://opsive.com/support/documentation/ultimate-character-controller/camera/view-types/included-view-types/first-person/)
- Holden: an offset spring (inertialization) lets you switch the follow target discontinuously (e.g., from animated head to ragdoll head) while the visible output stays continuous: `out_x = in_x + off_x` with `off_x` decaying through a critically damped spring. — [Spring-It-On](https://theorangeduck.com/page/spring-roll-call)
- GTA V exposes "First Person Ragdoll" as a toggle separate from "First Person Head Bobbing", i.e., the ragdoll-driven camera is treated as an intensity option. — [GTA BOOM](https://www.gtaboom.com/gta-5-first-person-guide-bc4b)

### Inferences (a reproducible recipe assembled from the cited pieces)
1. Base view = player yaw/pitch only (Titanfall/Oculus). Body orientation never writes into base view.
2. Camera anchor position = critically damped spring (Holden `simple_spring_damper_exact`) toward the simulated head position, halflife ≈ 0.05–0.12 s (lag 0.07–0.17 s, short enough to stay clear of the 0.2–0.4 Hz band); clamp the anchor–head offset to a small radius (e.g., ≤ 10–15 cm) as a safety limit (Opsive Min/Max Value) so the eyes never leave the skull. Let the visible neck/head mesh absorb the residual (Star Citizen counter-translation).
3. Impact layer = punch-angle spring with Source constants (k=65, c=9, impulse ×20) fed by (a) landing: roll = 0.013°·per u/s of impact speed (≈0.68° per m/s) above a threshold (~5.8 m/s), pitch capped at 8°; (b) wall hit: Quake-style direction-dependent kick, `roll ∝ (hitDir·right)`, `pitch ∝ (hitDir·forward)`, decaying in ≤0.5 s; (c) the impulse magnitude comes from Jolt's contact impulse on the head/torso bodies, so superhuman speed automatically produces bigger reactions — but clamp the summed punch to ≈12° total, matching the largest value Source ever produces.
4. When the ragdoll takes over (knockdown), switch the follow target from the animated head to the ragdoll head via an inertialization offset (Holden) so there is no snap, apply the Meta "soft collision" idea (decelerate camera before the wall) and, if the head is pushed inside geometry, fade/darken (VRTK) instead of teleporting the camera. Provide a "first-person ragdoll" toggle that falls back to a stabilised camera, as GTA V does.
5. Rotation from the body (ragdoll head roll) should be blended in at reduced gain (Star Citizen's counter-rotation removed ~80% of head rotation) and clamped; everything above the clamp is expressed by the visible body, not the camera.

### Gaps
- No developer source describes the camera attachment used by Boneworks/Bonelab, Blade & Sorcery, Gorn or Half-Life: Alyx (whether the camera is the physics head, and what happens on head–wall collision). The VRTK fade is the only documented mechanism found.
- No published numbers for acceptable camera translational displacement from a simulated head, nor for how much ragdoll rotation may be passed through.

## Key question 5: Procedural first-person arms and legs — hand IK to ledges, leaning/torso bend, Overgrowth-style pose interpolation applied to first-person rigs

### Takeaway
Shipped parkour games (Mirror's Edge, Dying Light) solved first-person body presence with large hand-authored animation sets and careful limb visibility rather than procedural IK, and they report that visible arms/feet are what stop the camera from feeling like "a floating camera". The procedural literature reachable here is limited to a general FP animation guide and middleware docs; the Overgrowth technique is documented only by its GDC abstract in the sources that could be opened.

### Cited Findings
- Mirror's Edge: "hundreds of first-person animations", ladder alone "over 40 animations ... jumping into it from multiple positions, climbing it up or down, or hanging on it with one hand"; "the visibility of Faith's arms and legs is used to convey her movement and interactions with the environment." — [Wikipedia: Mirror's Edge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mirror's_Edge) (citing Prima 2008 and IGN 2008)
- DICE considered attaching the camera to a mocapped head; it "didn't work out and good old animator talent was the solution." — [gameanim.com, 2010](https://www.gameanim.com/2010/11/05/creating-first-person-movement-for-mirrors-edge/)
- Dying Light: "we just have the main character's two arms, and sometimes his feet ... we had to get these limb animations ... perfected, because before that often you'd just feel like your character was a floating camera" (Binkowski); the grab button response was "slightly delayed" because players could not see the ledge edge (Kulon). — [MCV/Develop, 2016](https://mcvuk.com/development-news/the-vaulting-dead-implementing-first-person-parkour-in-dying-light/)
- Titanfall: lead animator Mark Grigsby — "Everything we're doing, we think about what it's going to look like when you do it" (gun position during slides, body contact with walls during runs). — [Game Developer, 2014](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/designer-interview-getting-i-titanfall-i-s-controls-just-right)
- Overgrowth: GDC 2014 "Animation Bootcamp: An Indie Approach to Procedural Animation", David Rosen (Wolfire Games): "how to use simple procedural techniques to achieve interactive and fluid animations using very few key frames, with examples from indie games like Overgrowth, Receiver and Black Shades." Breathing "12–18 cycles per minute at rest (~0.2–0.3 Hz)", recovering "over 3–8 seconds" after sprint. ### Inferences
- For a physics-driven body, the Dying Light delay trick generalises: the hand-IK reach to a ledge should begin from the physics prediction (time-to-ledge from velocity) so hands are visibly reaching *before* contact, giving the Oculus-style foreshadowing that makes the subsequent camera motion tolerable.
- Overgrowth-style interpolation between a handful of poses, driven by physics state (speed, lean = acceleration direction), is compatible with the "unified rig" approach: author 2–4 FP arm poses per locomotion state and blend by measured velocity/acceleration from Jolt, then add spring-based sway (Opsive/HL2 viewmodel bob constants) for high-frequency detail. Keep breathing/idle sway on the arms only, given its 0.2–0.3 Hz frequency (KQ3).
- Because all three parkour studios emphasise *visible limbs* over camera motion for body presence, budget effort toward arms/feet visibility (leg mesh in view when looking down, hands on walls during wall contact) rather than toward camera motion.
