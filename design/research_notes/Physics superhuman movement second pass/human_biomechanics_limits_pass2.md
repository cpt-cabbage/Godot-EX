# Human biomechanics limits and injury thresholds: second-pass verification and extension

Research date: 2026-09-27. Scope: re-verification of every number behind the pass-one note `human_biomechanics_limits.md` and the report sections "The biomechanics that set the thresholds", "The damage model" and "The three ladders", plus gap-filling and new sources. Every number carries a citation. Where a primary source could not be opened, the note says so and names what was used instead.

Access notes for this pass:

### 2h. Scaling and running-mechanics texts
- Dynamic similarity via the Froude number v²/(gL): "different mammals move in a dynamically similar fashion whenever they travel at speeds that give them equal values of ... the Froude number" — Alexander RMcN, Jayes AS (1983) J Zool 201:135–152 — [Crossref abstract](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1111/j.1469-7998.1983.tb04266.x).
- Bone safety factors: mammalian skeletons see peak locomotor stresses 25–50 % of failure strength, a safety factor of 2–4, kept roughly constant over 0.1–300 kg by more upright posture (higher muscle mechanical advantage), with allometry and reduced performance above that — Biewener AA (1990) Science 250:1097–1103 — [PubMed 2251499](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/2251499/). This is the principled statement the design needs: a superhuman whose forces rise N-fold without N-fold bone strength has a safety factor of (2–4)/N and fractures once N > 2–4 (consistent with the 3–5 m fracture band).
- Spring-mass running: Blickhan R (1989) J Biomech 22:1217–1227 — [PubMed 2625422](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/2625422/) (landing-velocity vector and leg length set the operating point; GRF ∝ specific contact time); McMahon TA, Cheng GC (1990) J Biomech 23 Suppl 1:65–78 — [PubMed 2081746](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/2081746/) (dimensionless horizontal Froude number U, vertical Froude number V and leg stiffness K_LEG; at high speed K_LEG is nearly linear in U and V; K_LEG = 15, V = 0.18 fits human data). For the game: scale gait with power by holding U and V constant, which makes stride length and contact time scale with √L and leg stiffness with body weight/leg length.
- McMahon 1984 (*Muscles, Reflexes, and Locomotion*) and Alexander 2003 (*Principles of Animal Locomotion*) were not opened; the two 1990/1989 papers above carry the equations.

## 3. New sources and findings

### 3a. Human running speed limit literature
- Top speed is limited by the minimum ground-contact time needed to apply the required mass-specific force, not by maximum force: at top speed forward running F_peak = 3.62 ± 0.24 BW and F_avg = 2.08 ± 0.07 BW with T_c = 0.108 ± 0.004 s, whereas one-legged hopping reaches 4.20 BW peak with 0.160 s contacts — Weyand PG, Sandell RF, Prime DN, Bundle MW (2010) J Appl Physiol 108(4):950–961 — [PubMed 20093666](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20093666/). Contribution: a speed tier should shorten contact time or raise force per contact; the ground force ceiling (≈ 3.6 BW per leg at top speed) is another number that must scale with durability.
- Removing the muscle force-velocity relationship in a forward-dynamics sprint model raises maximum speed by 15 %, removing all muscle properties by 22 %, while removing series elasticity lowers it by 26 % — Miller RH, Umberger BR, Caldwell GE (2012) J Biomech 45(6):1092–1097 — [PubMed 22035638](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22035638/). Contribution: "muscles without a force-velocity limit" alone buys only ≈ 15–22 % more speed; superhuman speed needs changed physics (force, contact time), which justifies treating speed tiers as separate from strength tiers.
- Force-velocity and ratio-of-force profiles of four elite (9.95–10.29 s) and five sub-elite sprinters are linear (R² ≈ 0.89–0.95) across a 40 m virtual sprint — Rabita G et al. (2015) Scand J Med Sci Sports 25(5):583–594 — [PubMed 25640466](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25640466/). Contribution: a linear F–v sprint model (F = F0 (1 − v/v0)) is empirically justified for the game's acceleration curve; numbers require the full text.

### 3b. Parkour landing literature
- Traceurs vs recreationally trained (10 + 10) from 25 % and 50 % body height: maximal vertical force 39.9 % and 49.0 % lower, loading rate 65.1 % and 66.3 % lower, time to peak 68.6 % and 65.9 % longer; forefoot/forefoot-midfoot strike in 93.2 % of traceur landings vs 8.3 % — Standing RJ, Maulder PS (2015) J Sports Sci Med 14(4):723–731 — [PubMed 26664268](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26664268/) (PMCID PMC4657414). Contribution: a "skill" multiplier of ≈ 0.5–0.6 on peak force and ≈ 0.35 on loading rate, independent of the roll.
- Grosprêtre & Lepers 2016 (§2a) supplies traceur jump capacities and greater eccentric knee-extensor torque.
- Recent (2020–2026) parkour papers are about training and fitness, not landing forces: weighted-vest training in novices (Eur J Sport Sci 2026, [PMC13576638](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13576638/)); parkour vs team-sport children (Front Sports Act Living 2025, [PMC11983557](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11983557/)); a triple Achilles injury case report in a parkour athlete (Clin Case Rep 2026, [PMC13451631](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13451631/)). No 2020s study measures cat-leap/arm-jump hand forces.

### 3c. Pedestrian meta-analysis
- Pooled 20 studies: odds of pedestrian death rise 11 % per km/h (OR 1.11, 95 % CI 1.10–1.12); fatality risk 5 % at 30 km/h, 10 % at 37, 50 % at 59, 75 % at 69, 90 % at 80 km/h — Hussain Q et al. (2019) Accid Anal Prev 129:241–249 — [PubMed 31176144](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31176144/). Contribution: a steeper, more conservative curve than Rosén & Sander (50 % at 77 km/h) and shallower than the pre-2000 curves; the game can bracket lethality between Rosén (lower) and Hussain (upper).

### 3d. Whole-body tolerance (Eiband curve)
- Voluntary limits vs plateau duration (full harness, ≈ 500 g/s onset): 45 g at 0.044 s, 40 g at 0.1 s, 25 g at 0.2 s, 13 g at 0.6 s, 10 g at 1.6 s; onset >1,000 g/s produces shock above 30 g; a building-fall survivor estimated at ≈ 200 g with moderate injury; lap-and-shoulder straps alone limit volunteers to 11.3 g at 0.28 s, thigh straps raise that to 32 g at 0.18 s — Eiband 1959 (§1c). Contribution: replaces the single "46 g" number with a duration-dependent tolerance, which is what an impulse-based damage model should implement (a_mean over Δt compared with the Eiband boundary rather than a fixed g).

### 3e. Recent fall biomechanics (2020–2026)
- Bodies can be deflected up to 1.5 m horizontally after the first ground impact from a high-rise fall and strike a second time (CCTV-documented double impact) — Bell LM et al. (2025) Int J Legal Med 139(4):1633–1638 — [PubMed 40133676](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40133676/). Contribution: ragdoll bounce and secondary impacts are physically real; the damage model should sum contacts.
- German level-1 centre: suicidal falls mean height 9.61 ± 6.42 m (ISS 32.0 ± 23.4) vs unintentional falls >3 m mean 6.14 ± 4.08 m (ISS 17.4 ± 14.0); severity difference persists at matched height (8.4 vs 8.5 m) — Zellner AA et al. (2025) Eur J Trauma Emerg Surg 51(1):345 — [PubMed 41288725](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41288725/). Contribution: intent (body orientation, no self-protection) is itself a multiplier, consistent with Casati's 3× and the head-first odds ratios.
- Landing from 10–60 cm: lower limb behaves first as a spring (ankle-dominated, sets peak force and loading rate) then as a spring-damper (knee-dominated, dissipates the energy) — Toussaint TD, Schepens B (2024) J Biomech 165:112014 — [PubMed 38422773](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38422773/). Contribution: a two-phase leg model (stiff spring then damper) is the right abstraction for a physics landing controller.

## 4. Contradictions found in the report or other notes, including recomputed tables

### 4a. Recomputed tables (all with m = 70 kg, g = 9.81 m/s²)

Kinetic energy, momentum, equivalent height. Report values 1,891 / 3,500 / 5,330 / 7,875 / 14,000 / 31,500 J at 7.35 / 10 / 12.34 / 15 / 20 / 30 m/s: **all correct** (recomputed 1,891, 3,500, 5,330, 7,875, 14,000, 31,500 J; momenta 514, 700, 864, 1,050, 1,400, 2,100 kg·m/s; equivalent heights 2.75, 5.10, 7.76, 11.47, 20.39, 45.87 m). "15–45 boxers' punches" = 1,891/121 = 15.6 and 5,330/121 = 44.0: correct.

Stopping distance d = v·0.22 + v²/(2a) with a = 5 and 8.5 m/s². Report 7.0/4.8, 12.2/8.1, 17.9/11.7, 25.8/16.5, 44.4/27.9, 96.6/59.5 m: **all correct**. Caveat: Harper's 6.86 m is measured from the start of braking, with no reaction distance, so the statement that it "sits between the two model rows, which validates the constants" compares unlike quantities. Without reaction time the model gives 5.4 m (a = 5) and 3.2 m (a = 8.5); 6.86 m from 7.35 m/s implies an effective uniform deceleration of 3.9 m/s², close to Harper's measured average of −4.44 m/s². Recommendation: use a_avg = 4.4 m/s² (not 5) for the "average braking" row; the peak 8.5 m/s² row is then a lower bound that no athlete sustains for a whole stop.

### 4b. Contradictions and misreadings that need editing in the report or notes
1. **Masonry is internally inconsistent in the report.** The biomechanics section says a human "cannot break masonry at all" and ladder S puts brick at S3 (20–50× a boxer's force), yet the same paragraph cites Mavrouli's 500 J and ladder H5 / ladder S / milestone 4 tune a brick wall to break at ≈ 270 N·s, with an unpowered sprinter (480 N·s) "marginal". By that criterion an unpowered 7.35 m/s sprinter (514 N·s, 1,891 J) already exceeds the boulder's momentum and energy nearly twice over, so the wall would break at tier zero. The resolution is physical: Mavrouli's impactor is a rigid 73 kg granite boulder that fails the wall by local shear over a small contact patch; a human body is compliant (0.1–0.3 m of chest or arm compression), distributes load, and the rigid striking mass is small (effective punch mass 2.9 kg, Walilko 2005). The comparator for a body is peak contact force/pressure, not whole-body kinetic energy. Recommendation: keep brick at S3, state that the 500 J / 270 N·s threshold applies to rigid impactors, and drive the Blast-style hardness test with the impulse of the rigid part actually in contact (an effective mass of a few kg for a fist or ≈ 5–10 kg for a shoulder, a game parameter) rather than whole-body momentum. Also reconcile masses: the destruction note uses 80 kg × 6 m/s = 480 N·s while the biomechanics note uses 70 kg × 7.35 m/s = 514 N·s.
2. **"Stapp's voluntary 46 g over 1.4 s"** overstates sustained tolerance by a factor of 3–4 (Eiband: 45 g only for 0.044 s; ≈ 10–13 g for 0.6–1.6 s). The damage model's "above 46 g blackout" should be duration-aware: compare a_mean over the contact duration with the Eiband boundary (45 g @ 0.044 s, 40 g @ 0.1 s, 25 g @ 0.2 s, 13 g @ 0.6 s). For the wall table this matters little (contacts are 7–80 ms) but it matters for landings and for any sustained superhuman acceleration or flight.
3. **Smith 2017 n** is 97, not 287 (pass-one note; the report cites only the LD50, which is right).
4. **Prinja 2020** is a destabilised-joint measurement; the pass-one note's "intact cadaveric shoulder, 123.57 N" should be struck and not used as a shoulder limit. The report does not cite it directly.
5. **Warner & Demling 1986** is Ann Emerg Med, not J Trauma, and does not state "LD50 ≈ 4 storeys"; the historical LD50 of 40 ft comes from Smith 2017's review of the literature.
6. **Harper deceleration distance range** is 2.39–7.93 m, not 2.9–7.93 m.
7. **Bolt's 0–10 m speed** of 5.26 m/s (Maćkała) includes reaction time; the IAAF laser value is 5.77 m/s. The pass-one inference "0–20 m in about 2.9 s, mean acceleration ≈ 3.5 m/s²" should read 2.73 s of running (2.88 − 0.146) and ≈ 3.7 m/s².
8. **Vigouroux attribution**: the "A2 load 36× higher in crimp than slope grip" figure belongs to Vigouroux et al. 2006 (J Biomech 39:2583), not Schweizer 2008.
9. **Report V0 row "≤ 2 m drops: 6.3 m/s, ~7 BW"** is the per-foot Niu value; per leg that is ≈ 4.8 kN, already above Funk's 3.7 kN threshold for a frail 65-year-old female specimen. Either label it "per foot, elderly-female threshold exceeded" or move the first fracture-risk warning to V0 for weak characters. This is consistent with the design's "start barely able to walk" premise: a frail starting body should be able to hurt itself from 2 m.
10. **Zhang 2004 thresholds** were flagged as "not verified in the paper"; two peer-reviewed citing papers now confirm the numbers (still not the paper itself).
11.
