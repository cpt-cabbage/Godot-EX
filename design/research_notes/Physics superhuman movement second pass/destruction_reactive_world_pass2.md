### 3.2 Real-time FEM and fracture research 2018–2026 (citation chains from Parker & O'Brien 2009 and Müller 2013 via Semantic Scholar, plus searches)

**Vertex Block Descent (VBD).** Chen, Liu, Yang, Yuksel, "Vertex Block Descent", ACM TOG 43(4), SIGGRAPH 2024 — [project PDF](https://graphics.cs.utah.edu/research/projects/vbd/vbd-siggraph2024.pdf); [arXiv 2403.06321](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.06321v3); [ACM](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3658179). A vertex-level Gauss–Seidel block coordinate descent on the variational implicit Euler form, "unconditionally stable", GPU-parallel with graph colouring; "We use a fixed frame time of 1/60 seconds"; all runs on an RTX 4090; scale examples include 216 squishy balls "totaling 48 million vertices and 151 million tetrahedra" (average 4.2 s per step — offline scale) down to real-time objects of a few thousand tets. Topology: "Our solution also works with other types of topological changes, such as tearing and fracturing. Deleting force elements does not require any changes to vertex coloring. When an object is split by duplicating vertices ... duplicated vertices can safely inherit the colors of their original vertices" (Fig. 10 tears a 2,500-vertex cloth). Follow-ups: Saillant, Zara, Jaillet, Damiand, "Going Further With Vertex Block Descent", Computer Animation and Virtual Worlds 2025 — extends VBD "to support any isoparametric elements and shape function orders", improves high-order performance and adds sub-stepping ([Semantic Scholar record, DOI 10.1002/cav.70039](https://api.semanticscholar.org/graph/v1/paper/DOI:10.1002/cav.70039); HAL and Wiley pages blocked). Giles (Roblox), Diaz, Yuksel, "Augmented Vertex Block Descent", ACM TOG 44(4), SIGGRAPH 2025 — augmented-Lagrangian hard constraints and rigid bodies; "a pile of 110,000 blocks smashed by a sphere using 4 iterations, taking 3.5 ms (9.8 ms including collision detection) per frame on an NVIDIA RTX 4090 GPU"; "35,000 rigid bodies connected with 72,000 joints falling onto a cloth"; and a brick-wall test where bricks "are attached via breakable hard constraints with a maximum force threshold": sequential impulse and XPBD fail to hold the constraints, "VBD bends, but does not break the wall. Our AVBD method produces plausible breaks" — [project PDF](https://graphics.cs.utah.edu/research/projects/avbd/Augmented_VBD-SIGGRAPH25.pdf); [ACM](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3731195); Real-Time Live! demo "Crazy Fast Physics!" ([ACM](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3721243.3735982)). Open implementations: NVIDIA/Google DeepMind/Disney Research's **Newton** (Linux Foundation, Apache-2.0, built on Warp, MuJoCo Warp as primary backend) ships VBD example solvers (`example_vbd_dat_rigid_soft`, `mujoco_franka_vbd_cable_admm_solver`) and a separate "Kamino" rigid solver — [Newton README](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/newton-physics/newton/main/README.md); [NVIDIA blog, 18 Mar 2025](https://developer.nvidia.com/blog/announcing-newton-an-open-source-physics-engine-for-robotics-simulation/); Warp itself is Apache-2.0 with a `warp.fem` module ([Warp README](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/NVIDIA/warp/main/README.md)).
Does this change the DMM verdict? **Partly — it changes the cost, not the recommendation.** VBD/AVBD make a DMM-class elastic solver (corotational or neo-Hookean tets, implicit, stable at 60 Hz) a solved, GPU-parallel problem with Python/Warp reference code, and VBD explicitly tolerates element deletion and vertex duplication, which is exactly Parker & O'Brien's node-splitting fracture. What is still missing for a DMM reproduction is unchanged: a crack criterion (separation tensor or per-constraint threshold), tet meshing, splinters/render skinning, sleeping islands, and — critically for this fork — it lives on the GPU in a separate solver, not in Jolt, so coupling to the Jolt character and rigid world is a second engineering problem. The FEM tier therefore stays "months, C++ (or CUDA/compute), only if the wood-and-steel bending fantasy is essential", with a lower research risk than pass one implied. AVBD's own brick-wall demo is a rigid-body-with-breakable-constraints model, i.e., the report's tier-3 bond graph, which supports choosing that tier first.

**Energized Rigid Body Fracture.** Li, Andrews, Jones, Bargteil, PACMCGIT 2018 (DOI 10.1145/3203207): rigid bodies with breakable constraints normally discard the elastic energy stored before failure; this method "computes the energy stored in these constraints when they are broken, and reintroduces it to the system as kinetic energy", giving "energetic fracture scenarios with results comparable to continuum mechanics approaches, but with the computational efficiency of rigid body simulation" — [Semantic Scholar record](https://api.semanticscholar.org/graph/v1/paper/DOI:10.1145/3203207) (OA PDF at mdsoar.org returned HTTP 400 this pass).
Does this change the DMM verdict? **It strengthens it.** Parker & O'Brien's stated reason for dynamic FEM ("rigid bodies joined by constraints produce deadened motions") has a published rigid-body-side fix: bank the bond strain energy and release it as an impulse on break — a few lines in a bond-graph implementation (Blast's "Split Impulse Strength" is the crude version).

**Bonded discrete element methods.** Lu, Li, Cao, Hu, "Simulating Fractures With Bonded Discrete Element Method", IEEE TVCG 2021: discrete particles joined by bonds; "efficient handling of multiple fractures, simple formulation and implementation, and good scaling consistency" but "high computational cost" ([record](https://api.semanticscholar.org/graph/v1/paper/DOI:10.1109/TVCG.2021.3106738)); Lu, Cao, Li, Hu, "Implicit Bonded Discrete Element Method with Manifold Optimization", ACM TOG (DOI 10.1145/3711852): implicit BDEM "2.1 to 9.8 times" faster than explicit BDEM, "better scale consistency and more realistic collision effects than finite element method and material point method" ([record](https://api.semanticscholar.org/graph/v1/paper/DOI:10.1145/3711852)). A GPU implementation of rod-bonded DEM with position-based dynamics exists in Rust/CUDA ("morningstar"), claiming to be "about 100x faster than the version in the paper" (self-reported, no licence shown) — [GitHub, entropylost/morningstar](https://github.com/entropylost/morningstar).
Does this change the DMM verdict? **No.** Research-grade, GPU, no game integration; interesting only as an alternative to FEM for "breaks into many pieces with correct energy".

**FEM fracture infrastructure.** Acharya & Hyde, "STA-FEM: Exact Streaming Assembly for Preplanned Dynamic Tetrahedral Topology Edits", arXiv 2605.11673 (May 2026): incremental solver-state updates after "fracture, refinement, or merge" edits, exactly equivalent to full rebuilds; "1.37x to 1.61x" end-to-end, "up to 76%" better fracture frame time, meshes up to 460 k elements — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.11673). Also in the chain: "Locking-Proof Tetrahedra" (TOG 2021), "Displacement-Correlated XFEM for Simulating Brittle Fracture" (CGF 2020), "Simulating Brittle Fracture with Material Points" (Fan, Chitalu, Komura, TOG 2022), "DeepFracture" (CGF 2025, learned fracture patterns), "Fast Nodal Hessian Computation for Peridynamic Fracture Simulation" (CGF 2026), "Dynamic Cutting Simulation Using Elastic Snapping" (CGF 2025) — all from the Semantic Scholar citation lists of [Parker & O'Brien 2009](https://api.semanticscholar.org/graph/v1/paper/DOI:10.1145/1599470.1599492/citations) and [Müller 2013](https://api.semanticscholar.org/graph/v1/paper/DOI:10.1145/2461912.2461934/citations); none report game-frame budgets.
Does this change the DMM verdict? **No.** They are offline or seconds-per-frame; they show that academic FEM fracture is still not a drop-in real-time library in 2026.

**Breaking Good (precomputed fracture modes).** Correct identifiers found: Sellán, Luong, Mattos Da Silva, Ramakrishnan, Yang, Jacobson, ACM TOG (DOI 10.1145/3549540), arXiv 2111.05249 (v1 9 Nov 2021, v5 4 Jul 2022) — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2111.05249); the ACM full text returned 403 and both author-site PDF guesses 404, so runtime timings are still not captured; licence remains non-commercial per the repository ([sgsellan/fracture-modes](https://github.com/sgsellan/fracture-modes)). Verdict effect: none.

**PBD-family fracture.** "Real-time voxelized mesh fracture with Gram–Schmidt constraints", Computers & Graphics 2025 (ScienceDirect PII S0097849325002237) is described in a search snippet as a stylised PBD destruction method "capable of running at hundreds of frames per second" — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0097849325002237) (403; abstract not opened, unverified). "PBI: Position-Based Dynamics Handles Updated Lagrangian Inelasticity" (arXiv 2405.11694) covers plasticity, not fracture ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.11694v1)). Verdict effect: none; PBD remains a deformation tool, not a fracture library.

**Survey and comparison papers.** Muguercia, Bosch, Patow, "Fracture modeling in computer graphics", Computers & Graphics 2014 (DOI 10.1016/j.cag.2014.08.006; 40 citations) — the standard taxonomy (mass-spring, FEM, meshless, geometric) ([record](https://api.semanticscholar.org/graph/v1/paper/DOI:10.1016/j.cag.2014.08.006); the udg.edu PDF fetched under that name is a different document). Thomas & Zhang, "Real-time fracturing in video games", Multimedia Tools and Applications 2022 (DOI 10.1007/s11042-022-13049-x): implements real-time fracture and compares it with pre-fracturing "in terms of visual realism and performance", then surveys participants; abstract only, PDF stub ([record](https://api.semanticscholar.org/graph/v1/paper/DOI:10.1007/s11042-022-13049-x)). Also in the chain, unopened: "A Comparison of Fracture Techniques For Use in Video Games" (2018) and "Rapid visual simulation of the progressive collapse of regular reinforced concrete frame structures based on machine learning and physics engine" (Wang et al., Engineering Structures 2023, 34 citations) — the latter is the closest thing found to a validated "building collapse look" model. **Convex decomposition upgrade.** Wei et al., "Approximate convex decomposition for 3D meshes with collision-aware concavity and tree search" (CoACD), ACM TOG 2022 (170 citations in the Müller 2013 chain): MIT licence, C++ with Python bindings and Unity support, aims "to preserve collision conditions of the input shape with fewer components" — [GitHub, SarahWeiii/CoACD](https://github.com/SarahWeiii/CoACD). ## 4. Contradictions found in the report or other notes

1. **Brick-wall threshold versus the masonry gating (report §"Each power arrives one tier before the body can survive it" and the biomechanics note).** The report's S ladder says a human "cannot break masonry at all" and puts masonry at S3 ("20–50× a boxer's 3.4 kN"), citing the biomechanics note's brick compressive strength of 78 MPa ([BIA TN 3A](https://www.gobrick.com/media/file/TN_3A_Brick_Masonry_Material_Properties.pdf)); but the same section, following the destruction note, sets a brick wall's Hardness so that "~270 N·s (73 kg at 3.7 m/s) breaks it", calls an unpowered sprinter at "≈ 480 N·s" *marginal*, and the H ladder's H5 row has the wall break only at 30 m/s+. Arithmetic: 480 N·s is 1.8 times the 270 N·s break impulse (and 1,891 J at 7.35 m/s is 3.8 times Mavrouli's 500 J), so by the report's own rule an unpowered sprinter already breaks the wall at H0/S0, contradicting both "cannot break masonry" and the H5 placement. The root cause is mixing two failure criteria: Mavrouli et al. measured a hard, compact 72.9 kg granite boulder punching through a **single-leaf, unreinforced 1 m × 1 m** wall by "local shear damage" ([Mavrouli 2024](https://doi.org/10.3390/geosciences14090226)), whereas the biomechanics note gates on crushing brick units. A human body is a compliant, distributed impactor whose energy goes into the body (the report's own 27.5 g / 18.9 kN chest numbers), so the boulder threshold should apply only to hard projectiles (thrown objects, ground-pound, an armoured fist) and body charges into masonry should stay injury-limited until a superhuman durability tier. The report should state the wall type and impactor type next to the 270 N·s number.
2. **Player mass.** The destruction note's arithmetic uses 80 kg (480 N·s at 6 m/s; 4 kJ at 10 m/s); the report uses 70 kg everywhere else (1,891 J at 7.35 m/s) yet repeats "≈ 480 N·s" for the sprinter. At 70 kg × 7.35 m/s the impulse is 515 N·s; at 70 kg × 6 m/s it is 420 N·s. Pick one mass.
3. **Quantum Break as a DMM shipping title.** Report: DMM "shipped in The Force Unleashed I and II and Quantum Break". Per the Pixelux release and fxguide (section 1, row 22), Quantum Break used the DMM *Playback System* (baked simulations, GPU vertex-animation playback in Northlight). The sentence should read "...and was used as an offline authoring/playback tool in Quantum Break"; the last runtime DMM title is TFU II (2010), which strengthens the "assume no active runtime SDK" conclusion.
4. **`Mesh.convex_decompose()` is not a script API** (note and report). Only `MeshInstance3D.create_multiple_convex_collisions()` is exposed; a fork binding would be needed to decompose shards from GDScript without instantiating a MeshInstance3D.
5. **"Fairly expensive" misattribution** (note, KQ2 and KQ6). The Jolt docs say dynamic *mesh shapes* are "fairly expensive to simulate"; no such statement exists about soft bodies. The report's wording ("Jolt's and Bullet's soft bodies are position-based and do not fracture") is fine; the note's quotation should be fixed.
6. **Jolt speed cap versus the H6 "Mach regimes" and Megaton-style flight** (report ladders and Megaton section). The fork clamps every body's linear speed to `physics/jolt_physics_3d/limits/max_linear_velocity` = 500 m/s (and angular to 2700°/s) and sizes the world boundary at 2000 m by default (`jolt_project_settings.cpp:68–70`; applied in `jolt_body_3d.cpp:110`). Mach 1 is ~343 m/s and the report's Megaton reference reaches "Mach 8 in about five seconds". Nothing in the report or notes mentions raising these settings (or using a kinematic/`CharacterVirtual` path above the cap). Not a source contradiction, but a hard engine constraint the design must address in the same place it discusses `precision=double`.
7. **Determinism attribution** (report tier text). The caveat is sourced to the deprecated godot-jolt extension README; the official "Using Jolt Physics" page is silent, and Jolt's next release removes the determinism toggle ("always deterministic").
