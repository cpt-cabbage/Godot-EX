**A quantified camera-roll tolerance literature (conflicting).**
- Oh & Son 2022 (*Sensors* 22(4):1314, [doi:10.3390/s22041314](https://doi.org/10.3390/s22041314); 154 valid participants, 52 VR scenes, HMD): mean opinion score for **roll** scenes (3.039) was "43.0 % and 4.7 % higher than those of" yaw and pitch respectively; translation acceleration raised sickness 19.7 % over constant velocity (p < 0.001); vertical translation ≈16–18 % worse than forward; fast translation at 9.2 m/s ≈27.5 % worse than moderate speed; giving the user control reduced MOS by 22.5 %; only a strongly restricted FOV (30°) produced a meaningful (~6 %) reduction ([PMC8963115](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8963115/)).
- Lo & So 2001 (*Applied Ergonomics*, [doi:10.1016/S0003-6870(00)00059-4](https://doi.org/10.1016/s0003-6870(00)00059-4), PMID 11209825): 16 men, four 20-min sessions, scene oscillation in pitch, yaw, roll or none at "30 degrees/s, range: +/- 60 degrees"; all oscillation axes increased nausea and SSQ versus none, but "the overall effects of axis among our group of 16 randomly selected participants were not significant".
- Bonato, Bubka & Palmisano 2009 (*Aviat Space Environ Med*, [doi:10.3357/asem.2394.2009](https://doi.org/10.3357/asem.2394.2009), PMID 19911517): 19 subjects, cube rotating at 60°/s about pitch or pitch+roll; dual-axis rotation gave significantly higher SSQ total, nausea, oculomotor and disorientation after 5 min.
- Terenzi & Zaal 2020 (NASA Ames / SJSU, *AIAA SciTech 2020*, [doi:10.2514/6.2020-0171](https://doi.org/10.2514/6.2020-0171), [NTRS PDF](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20200000787/downloads/20200000787.pdf)): staircase-detected **onset** thresholds for a particle field in an HTC Vive Pro, optic flow equalised across conditions; Table 4 means (paper's units per its nomenclature: m/s for translation, rad/s for rotation; m/s² and rad/s² for the accelerating cases): forward 1.183, lateral 0.890, **roll 0.364, yaw 0.473** (steady); forward 0.700, lateral 0.586, roll 0.474, yaw 0.478 (accelerating). Rotational thresholds were significantly lower than translational (constant velocity: M = 0.415 vs 1.035, p < 0.001; acceleration: 0.476 vs 0.715, p = 0.017); **roll vs yaw was not significant** (p = 0.533 and 0.946). Standard deviations are of the order of the means, and the authors conclude "the high variability between individuals implies that the individual tuning of motion controls would be more effective". If the units are rad/s as the nomenclature states, the onset thresholds are ≈21°/s (roll) and ≈27°/s (yaw), which is below the 30°/s used by Lo & So.
- Allison & Palmisano 2025 (*Multisensory Research*, [doi:10.1163/22134808-bja10181](https://doi.org/10.1163/22134808-bja10181), pp. 1–105; 97 studies from 4,622 records, searched Nov 2024): "peripheral field of view restriction and independent visual backgrounds were consistently associated with reductions in cybersickness severity. Conversely, visual oscillation, multidimensional visual motion stimuli, and visually simulated off-vertical axis rotation were found to exacerbate cybersickness."
- Net: roll is provocative and multi-axis rotation is worse than single-axis, but whether roll is worse than yaw at equal optic flow is contested (Oh & Son yes; Lo & So and Terenzi & Zaal no). None of these is a flat-screen study, and none gives a "safe degrees of roll" number; the best design reading is that the *rate and duration* of roll matter more than a static angle, and that onset in an HMD begins around 20–30°/s of sustained rotation.

## 3. New sources and findings

Each entry says what it contributes to the camera design.

**Meta-analyses and reviews**
- Saredakis, Szpak, Birckhead, Keage, Rizzo & Loetscher 2020, "Factors Associated With Virtual Reality Sickness in Head-Mounted Displays: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis", *Frontiers in Human Neuroscience* 14:96, [doi:10.3389/fnhum.2020.00096](https://doi.org/10.3389/fnhum.2020.00096): 55 studies, 3,016 participants; gaming content had the highest total SSQ (34.26, 95 % CI 29.57–38.95) versus a pooled 28.00; **controller-based locomotion had the highest locomotion-type SSQ (M = 32.55)**; nausea and disorientation were lower for exposures under 10 min; older samples (≥35 y) scored lower; no sex difference. Contribution: quantifies why stick-driven movement is the riskiest class and why session length matters for onboarding.
- Caserman, Garcia-Agundez, Gámez Zerban & Göbel 2021, "Cybersickness in current-generation virtual reality head-mounted displays: systematic review and outlook", *Virtual Reality* 25:1153–1170, [doi:10.1007/s10055-021-00513-6](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10055-021-00513-6): 49 publications 2013–2019; current-generation HMDs cause significantly less sickness (p < 0.001) "some symptoms remain as intense"; "the nature of movement and, in particular, sensory mismatch as well as perceived motion have been the leading c[auses]". Contribution: hardware improvements did not remove the content-side problem.
- Chang, Kim & Yoo 2020, "Virtual Reality Sickness: A Review of Causes and Measurements", *International Journal of Human–Computer Interaction* 36(17), [doi:10.1080/10447318.2020.1778351](https://doi.org/10.1080/10447318.2020.1778351): metadata only (Crossref; 908 citations); abstract not retrievable (T&F 403).
- Allison & Palmisano 2025 (see §2): FOV restriction and independent visual backgrounds help; oscillation, multi-axis motion and off-vertical rotation hurt.
- Keshavarz, Riecke, Hettinger & Campos 2015, "Vection and visually induced motion sickness: how are they related?", *Frontiers in Psychology* 6:472, [doi:10.3389/fpsyg.2015.00472](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2015.00472): whether vection is a prerequisite for VIMS "is still a matter of debate". Contribution: do not assume that suppressing vection alone (e.g. by speed lines) removes sickness.
- Weech, Kenny & Barnett-Cowan 2019, "Presence and Cybersickness in Virtual Reality Are Negatively Related: A Review", *Frontiers in Psychology* 10:158, [doi:10.3389/fpsyg.2019.00158](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.00158). Contribution: comfort measures that preserve presence (subtle FOV restriction) are preferable to ones that break it.

**FOV restrictor and optic-flow work**
- Fernandes & Feiner 2016, "Combating VR sickness through subtle dynamic field-of-view modification", *IEEE 3DUI 2016* (Best Paper), [doi:10.1109/3DUI.2016.7460053](https://doi.org/10.1109/3DUI.2016.7460053), [PDF](https://www.cs.columbia.edu/2016/combating-vr-sickness/images/combating-vr-sickness.pdf): 30 seated participants, Oculus DK2, gamepad locomotion, two sessions; a soft-edged circular restrictor contracts with virtual translation/rotation speed. Pilot calibration: participants first noticed an FOV change at **95°** (mode; range 75–107.5°), judged hard cutouts to detract from the experience at **80°** (mode), and preferred a minimum FOV of **90°** (mode; 75–100°); the study used 80° and 90° minimum FOVs. "11 of 15 R-90 participants marked a 1 ('Did not notice or did not happen') for FOV change"; the restrictor lowered the running discomfort score (Wilcoxon Z = −2.75, p = 0.003) with no significant SSQ or presence difference. Contribution: a velocity-driven vignette that stays above ~90° is imperceptible to most players and still helps; the report's optional "speed vignette" can cite these numbers.
- Wu & Suma Rosenberg 2022, "Adaptive Field-of-view Restriction: Limiting Optical Flow to Mitigate Cybersickness in Virtual Reality", *ACM VRST 2022*, [doi:10.1145/3562939.3565611](https://doi.org/10.1145/3562939.3565611): restrictor driven by measured optical flow rather than speed (metadata and search summary only).
- Nie, Duh, Liu & Wang 2020, "Analysis on Mitigation of Visually Induced Motion Sickness by Applying Dynamical Blurring on a User's Retina", *IEEE TVCG* 26(8), [doi:10.1109/TVCG.2019.2893668](https://doi.org/10.1109/TVCG.2019.2893668): the only peer-reviewed motion-blur/comfort study surfaced; not opened (metadata via Crossref). Contribution: motion blur is studied as a *mitigation* in VR, while flat-screen players in the pass-one ResetEra/Busseneau sources disable it; the two are not the same effect (retinal blur of peripheral flow vs. full-frame camera blur).
- So, Lo & Ho 2001, "Effects of navigation speed on motion sickness caused by an immersive virtual environment", *Human Factors* 43(3), [doi:10.1518/001872001775898223](https://doi.org/10.1518/001872001775898223), PMID 11866200: 96 men, 30-min head-steered VE, RMS speeds 3, 4, 6, 8, 10, 24, 30, 59 m/s; "Both the nausea and vection ratings increased significantly with speeds increasing from 3 m/s to 10 m/s. At speeds exceeding 10 m/s, the ratings stabilized"; speed changed onset time, not the rate of increase. **Contribution (directly relevant to the Megaton Rainfall end state): above ~10 m/s, further speed did not add nausea in this study; the risk is concentrated in the 3–10 m/s band that the early game lives in, and in accelerations.**
- Bonato, Bubka, Palmisano & Phillip 2008, "Vection Change Exacerbates Simulator Sickness in Virtual Environments", *Presence* 17(3):283–292, [doi:10.1162/pres.17.3.283](https://doi.org/10.1162/pres.17.3.283): a steadily expanding flow field produced significantly less sickness than one alternately expanding and contracting; "changing vection exacerbates SS". Contribution: laboratory support for Oculus's "acceleration, not velocity" rule and for the report's argument against oscillatory camera lag.
- Terenzi & Zaal 2020 (see §2): rotational optic flow reaches onset at far lower rates than translational flow; the paper also notes "jittering or a series of high-frequency accelerations of the visual field have been shown to induce strong motion sickness".
- Oh & Son 2022 (see §2): controllability −22.5 %, acceleration +19.7 %, vertical translation +16–18 %.
- Palmisano, Allison & Kim 2020, "Cybersickness in Head-Mounted Displays Is Caused by Differences in the User's Virtual and Physical Head Pose", *Frontiers in Virtual Reality* 1:587698, [doi:10.3389/frvir.2020.587698](https://doi.org/10.3389/frvir.2020.587698). Contribution: HMD-specific (display lag); relevant only if a VR mode is ever added, but it is the theoretical reason any body-driven offset of a VR camera is intolerable.

**Platform guidance**
- Meta Horizon "Locomotion comfort and usability" and "Locomotion Best Practices", both last updated 2025-12-17 ([comfort](https://developers.meta.com/horizon/design/locomotion-comfort-usability/); [best practices](https://developers.meta.com/horizon/design/locomotion-best-practices/)): walking ~3 mph/1.4 m/s, running ~6 mph/2.8 m/s; snap angles 15/30/45/90/180°; soft collisions; consistent camera elevation; vignettes "to darken or occlude screen edges during movement, limiting visible optic flow"; "Keep acceleration events brief and infrequent"; "Offer smooth turning as an opt-in feature, tuning speed and acceleration carefully"; ship three presets — "Recommended (best overall), Comfortable (prioritizes comfort for motion-sensitive users), and Advanced (full control for experienced users)"; "visible avatar that indicates upcoming camera movements can significantly enhance comfort". Contribution: the preset structure is a ready-made settings design for the report's "low-intensity default".

**Practitioner and developer material**

- Titanfall: landing spring reuses the weapon-recoil spring (see 1g). Contribution: one additive spring, two excitation sources — the same architecture the report proposes for layer three.
- Source `cl_smoothtime 0.1` and the HL1 `pm_shared.c` lineage (see 1b–1c). Contribution: Valve's own "smooth the eye after a discontinuity over 0.1 s" is a shipped value for the report's inertialization blend; the landing-roll formula has survived unchanged since 1998, which is evidence of its tolerability rather than a Source-specific tuning.

## 4. Contradictions found in the report or other notes

**D1. "view roll default to zero in Quake, Half-Life and Source" (report, comfort paragraph) is wrong for Quake.** Quake ships `cl_rollangle "2.0"` and `cl_rollspeed "200"`, so strafing rolls the view 2° by default ([view.c lines 41–42](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/id-Software/Quake/master/WinQuake/view.c)); the pass-one note itself lists this value. Only Source's `sv_rollangle` defaults to `"0"`. Half-Life 1's `rollangle` is an engine cvar (`movevars->rollangle`) whose default is not in the SDK, so the HL1 claim is not verifiable from the sources cited. Fix: "view roll defaults to 2° in Quake and to 0 in Source; idle sway defaults to 0 in all three."

**D2. "Valve moved away from camera motion ... and onto the viewmodel" is only true of HL2/Source.** Quake adds the bob to the eye origin (view.c line 893) and Half-Life 1 still does (view.cpp line 522 `pparams->vieworg[2] += ( bob );`) while also bobbing the viewmodel. The pass-one inference "the HL1/HL2 successors apply bob mainly to the viewmodel" is wrong for HL1. The historical trend statement survives (Quake and HL1 bob the eye; HL2 does not) but the wording should say so.

**D3. Roystan exponent.** The report says "the common Unity port uses exponent 2"; the tutorial's final tuned settings set "Trauma Exponent to 1" and it introduces 2 only as the field default ([roystan.net](https://roystan.net/articles/camera-shake/)). Eiserloh's own recommendation is 2 or 3, so the design value should be cited to Eiserloh, not to Roystan.

**D4. Running speed citation.** "2.8–3 m/s running" is cited to the Oculus PDF, which says only 3 m/s; 2.8 m/s comes from the Meta Horizon web page. Cite both.

**D5. Landing pitch cap.** The report says landing produces roll "with pitch hard-capped at 8°", implying landing writes pitch. In both HL1 and Source the landing *sets* roll only and the 8° cap clamps a pitch punch that already exists ([gamemovement.cpp `PlayerRoughLandingEffects`](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/ValveSoftware/source-sdk-2013/master/src/game/shared/gamemovement.cpp); [pm_shared.c lines 2735–2739](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/ValveSoftware/halflife/master/pm_shared/pm_shared.c)). The report's "clamp the summed punch to about 12° total" is a design inference, not Source behaviour (Source clamps at ±89°/±179°).

**D6. Quotation marks around paraphrases.** Three Titanfall quotes in the report and the pass-one note are paraphrases ("with intensity proportional to fall height"; "as if the character was constantly looking side-to-side"; "immediately improved the experience"); the verbatim text is in §1g. Meaning is preserved in all three.

**D7. "Every studio that tried a head-mounted camera reports the same failure."** Only DICE (via a summary by a third party) and CIG are sourced. Rockstar (GTA V) and Warhorse (Kingdom Come) shipped head-bone-driven first-person cameras; Rockstar with three opt-out toggles, Warhorse without one and with the resulting complaints ([KCD forum](https://forum.kingdomcomerpg.com/t/head-bobbing-motion-sickness/39868)). "Every studio that spoke about it" is defensible; "every studio" is not.

**D8. Attribution of the DICE finding.** The gameanim.com sentence is Jonathan Cooper's paraphrase of the 2009 GDC talk ("It's interesting to see someone else thought of attaching a camera to a mocapped head..."), not a DICE quotation. The report presents it as DICE's own words.

**D9. Tension with `procedural_animation_techniques.md` (line 232).** That note describes Fabrice Piquet's UE4 "true first-person" approach — "camera attached to the head socket so camera motion comes from the skeleton" — without a comfort caveat, while the camera note and report argue that a head-driven camera is the highest-risk configuration. Both can be true (Piquet's head is animated, not simulated, and the report's claim is specifically about a *simulated* head), but the report should say why a hand-animated head socket is acceptable and a Jolt head body is not: the animator can author the head path to be smooth, the solver cannot.

**D10. Roll-tolerance wording in the report should not over-claim.** The report says "roll tolerance limits ... are not quantified in any source found" (pass-one gap); the new sources partly fill this but disagree with each other (§2). The report's rule "opt-in, clamp to 8–12°, decay within 0.5–1 s" remains an inference; the new evidence supports the *rate* framing (onset around 20–30°/s sustained; multi-axis worse than single-axis) more than any static angle.

**D11. de Vries/Bos inference.** The report's "expose a wide FOV range and do not tie bob amplitude to FOV" is fine, but the report omits the authors' own caveat that the congruent-is-worse result "may be explained by the relative large differences between IFOV and EFOV we used" and that absolute FOV size showed no significant effect in their data. The FOV-comfort literature in HMDs (Fernandes & Feiner; Oh & Son; Allison & Palmisano) points the other way (narrower effective FOV during motion is more comfortable), so the two findings must be presented as display-specific rather than as one rule.

**D12. Consistency with `first_person_movement_controllers.md`.** That note's HL1/HL2 fall thresholds (1024/580/200/350 and 922.5/526.5/173/303) match this pass's re-check exactly; no contradiction.
